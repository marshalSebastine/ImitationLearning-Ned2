# Narrow forward-looking wrist camera on sharp turns: memory, active vision, camera placement and policy design for imitation-learned puck pushing

*Use case carried through every judgement: Niryo Ned2 pushes a 2.4 cm red puck along a 1 cm black line on A4 with only the wrist-link Vision Set camera. Camera looks straight ahead (away from base) whenever the pusher rod is vertical; window about 9 cm wide, 7 cm ahead of the puck; puck always at the bottom of the frame; line ahead visible only while the path heads within about 45 degrees of "away from base". Camera yaw by forearm roll works only near one spot (about 20 cm from base); a hand-mounted camera would rotate with the pusher but is limited to +-145 degrees. 1 cm step per second. Imitation learning only, two beginners, 100 to 300 demonstrations. Test sheets: arc, S-curve with hairpin, meander, self-crossing loops; final path unseen. Research date: 2026-10-25.*

## Key question 1: Policies with memory (frame stacking, LSTM/GRU, transformer history, ACT, Diffusion Policy, RT-1) and the copycat / causal-confusion pitfall

### Takeaway
Short histories (2 frames) are the standard default and longer naive frame stacks usually hurt vision-based policies; recurrent (BC-RNN) or specially trained long-context diffusion policies do solve tasks that require remembering something that has left the view, but only with a fix for the copycat problem (auxiliary past-action prediction, or avoiding history entirely via action chunking). With 100 to 300 demos the evidence base (robomimic 200 demos, PTP 50 to 200 demos) is in the right regime, but the gains in image-based copycat mitigation are still described as an open problem.

### Cited Findings
- Copycat problem definition (NeurIPS 2020): "the imitator learns to cheat by predicting the expert's previous action, rather than the next action", occurring "in partially observed settings when expert actions are strongly correlated over time"; the resulting policy "never switches actions when it should". Six MuJoCo tasks with velocities masked to create partial observability. — [Wen et al. 2020, Fighting Copycat Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14876)
- Wen et al. 2020 numbers (cumulative reward, BC single-obs / BC obs-history / their adversarial method): Ant 1300 / 1750 / 2150; Hopper 275 / 293 / 1086; Humanoid 587 / 565 / 671; Walker2d 363 / 592 / 1296; HalfCheetah -38 / 820 / 1250; Reacher -79 / -64 / -54. History helped plain BC "in five out of six environments" but far less than with the copycat fix. — [Wen et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2010.14876v1)
- Same paper on images: "the improvement in image-based experiments is not significant yet"; image-based causal confusion listed as future work. — [Wen et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2010.14876v1)
- de Haan et al. 2019 "causal confusion": imitation policies attribute expert actions to nuisance correlates (the previous action being the common one); their CCIL method needs disentangled representations and online environment interventions, could not run on high-dimensional observations (as described by Wen et al.; CCIL scores Hopper 145, Walker2d 474, HalfCheetah 714). — [de Haan et al. 2019, Causal Confusion in Imitation Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11979); description from [Wen et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2010.14876v1)
- robomimic (CoRL 2021, 200 proficient-human demos per task, 300 multi-human): Lesson L1 "Models with temporal abstraction can be extremely effective in learning from human datasets"; Sec. 4.1 "Observation history is crucial for good performance". Image-based BC vs BC-RNN success (%): Square PH 62.0 vs 82.0; Transport PH 55.3 vs 72.0; Tool Hang PH 20.0 vs 67.3; Square MH 46.0 vs 76.7. Sequence lengths {10, 30, 50} compared: "We found longer sequence length does not improve performance significantly". — [Mandlekar et al. 2021, What Matters in Learning from Offline Human Demonstrations](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.03298)
- Diffusion Policy (RSS 2023, extended arXiv version): default observation horizon To=2, action horizon Ta=8, prediction horizon Tp=16; "the vision-based variant, in particular the CNN backbone one, sees performance decrease as observation horizon increases. In practice, they found an observation horizon of 2 is good for most tasks". — [Chi et al. 2023, Diffusion Policy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04137)
- Diffusion Transformer Policy (2024) and Dita (2025) on ManiSkill2: performance "significantly drops when we increase the length of observation history to 3"; 2 frames best, mainly helpful when the prediction horizon is long. — [DiT Policy](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.15959); [Dita](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.19757)
- ACT / ALOHA (RSS 2023): policy conditioned on the current observation only; action chunking handles non-Markovian pauses "without introducing the causal confusion issue for history-conditioned policies"; chunk size 100, 50 demos per task, real-task success 20 to 96 %. — [Zhao et al. 2023, ACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.13705)
- Past-Token Prediction (2025): with a 16-step context, naive long-context diffusion collapses (Square 0.17, Tool-Hang 0.00, Transport 0.00, long-horizon ALOHA 0.20, long-horizon Square 0.03), no-history gets 0.79 / 0.51 / 0.60 / 0.28 / 0.12, and PTP gets 0.89 / 0.75 / 0.67 / 0.98 / 0.93. Memory tasks: long-horizon Square (100 demos) and ALOHA (50 demos) built so the goal cannot be inferred from the current frame. Real-world (50 to 200 demos): no-history about 15 %, naive long context near zero on three of four tasks, PTP about 70 %. The failure of naive long context is attributed to "spurious correlations"; the authors find modern diffusion policies show the reverse of copycat: they "fail to capture essential dependencies between past and future actions". — [Torne et al. 2025, Learning Long-Context Diffusion Policies via Past-Token Prediction](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09561v2)
- Long-context diffusion re-evaluation (2026): "naively scaling context length is not as brittle as advertised in literature" when using a UNet with cross-attention conditioning; also "These policies cannot solve tasks that require memory and can get stuck repeatedly executing the same failing motions" (motivation for memory). — [Training and Evaluating Diffusion Policies with Long Context Lengths, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16447)
- RT-1 (2022) takes "a short history of images from a robot's camera" (secondary sources give 6 frames). — [Google Research blog on RT-1](https://research.google/blog/rt-1-robotics-transformer-for-real-world-control-at-scale/)
- Mobile-robot evidence that a feed-forward CNN fails when the turn needs context: an NTNU 2018 thesis found indoor lane following worked, but a U-turn task "which needs some notion of history, succeeded only occasionally". — [NTNU thesis, End-to-End Steering Angle Prediction](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2566504)

### Inferences
- Inference: at 1 cm per step, a 2-frame stack covers 2 cm of travel; a hairpin that takes the line out of the 7 cm window for the duration of a 180 degree turn (roughly 10 to 20 steps for a turn radius of a few cm) is far outside what To=2 can bridge. A BC-RNN (robomimic sequence length 10 = 10 cm) or a PTP-style 16-step context (16 cm) is the right order of magnitude; longer than that is unsupported by evidence and costs data.
- Inference: this task is a textbook copycat setting. Expert actions are highly autocorrelated ("keep pushing forward" dominates the data), the observation is partial (line leaves view), and the puck at the bottom of every frame makes the previous action partly recoverable from the image. A naive history policy would likely learn "repeat the last step", which at a hairpin means pushing straight off the line. Any history design must include a mitigation: ACT-style chunking without history, or PTP-style auxiliary past-action prediction, or at minimum never feeding the previous action as an explicit input.
- Inference: for two beginners, ACT (current observation + chunk) or Diffusion Policy with To=2 is the lowest-risk baseline; BC-RNN (robomimic) is the next step with the best 200-demo evidence for memory; PTP is the strongest memory method but adds a multistage recipe. All three have public LeRobot/robomimic code.
- Inference: the 2026 long-context paper suggests the brittleness of naive history is partly an architecture artefact (UNet + cross-attention conditioning); but the task-specific memory results with sub-200 demos still come only from PTP and BC-RNN.

### Gaps
- No paper tested a history policy on a "target leaves a narrow wrist view during a turn and comes back" task directly; the closest analogues are the PTP memory tasks (goal hidden from the current frame) and the NTNU U-turn result.
- Image-based copycat mitigation gains are explicitly called "not significant yet" by Wen et al.; no later paper quantified copycat for image-based manipulation with 100 to 300 demos.
- The exact robomimic BC-RNN final sequence length could not be confirmed from the visible text (10 is widely used in the released configs but not verified here).

## Key question 2: Active perception and camera control as part of the action (learning to look, AV-ALOHA, ViA, EyeRobot, gaze actions); is a camera yaw action sensible here?

### Takeaway
Camera motion can be learned purely by imitation as extra action dimensions in the same ACT or diffusion policy (AV-ALOHA: 7 extra joints, 50 demos per task; ViA: neck pose in a 23-dim action), and it helps a lot when the fixed/wrist views lose the target (ViA +45 % average; AV-ALOHA Hidden Pick 60 % vs 35 % wrist-only), but it hurts when the fixed views already suffice and it needs camera-motion demonstrations (VR headset). A yaw action is sensible for this task only if the camera is mounted so that yaw does not tilt the pusher (hand mount), and the demos must contain the human's look-ahead behaviour.

### Cited Findings
- AV-ALOHA (2024, v2 March 2025): extends ALOHA 2 with a 7-DoF arm carrying only a stereo camera; the operator's VR headset (Meta Quest 2/3) head motion drives the camera arm during teleoperation; policy is ACT from LeRobot with the action "expanded to predict 21 joint positions per timestep to accommodate the additional 7-DoF AV arm"; 50 demos per task. — [Chuang et al., Active Vision Might Be All You Need](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.17435v2)
- AV-ALOHA results (success %, columns: Peg Ins., Slot Ins., Hook Pkg., Pour Tube, Thread Needle, Occluded Ins. real, Hidden Pick real): AV 42/50/22/14/52/20/55; AV+Wrist 34/44/22/14/52/30/60; Static 48/66/44/8/30/20/40; Static+Wrist 40/78/30/6/22/15/35; Wrist only 42/44/8/10/44/15/35; all cameras "never ranked in the top three for any task". "For those cases where AV is not necessarily advantageous, the added complexity from variation in viewpoint can negatively impact performance." Limitations: larger action space, "susceptibility to distribution shift", may need "more training data". — [AV-ALOHA](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.17435v2)
- Vision in Action (ViA, 2025): humans teleoperate while seeing the robot's own camera view through a headset, so their gaze strategy is recorded; the policy outputs "future end-effector poses of the neck and arms" (neck pose part of a 23-dim action), 10 Hz, prediction horizon 16, execution 8; 125 to 260 demos per task; "improve average task performance by 45%" over wrist/fixed-camera baselines; wrist cameras fail because "their viewpoints are constrained by manipulation requirements rather than guided by perceptual objectives" and can be fully occluded. — [Xiong et al. 2025, Vision in Action](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15666v1)
- EyeRobot (2025): gaze policy learned with RL inside a BC perception-action loop rather than from camera demonstrations. — [EyeRobot](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10968)
- Look, Focus, Act (2025): gaze treated as part of the action space, predicted jointly with arm actions, with foveated vision; reports better precision and distractor robustness. — [Look, Focus, Act](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.15833v1)
- TAVIS benchmark (May 2026): head-camera and wrist-camera active-vision suites; Diffusion Policy and pi0 baselines; active vision "helps on average but unevenly across tasks" and "imitation alone can produce anticipatory gaze" (from the search summary of the abstract). — [TAVIS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.07943)
- Hsu et al. 2022: in the low-observability peg-insert-side-hard task the hand-camera-only agent eventually succeeded "by zooming out to improve its observability" (the paper covers both IL and RL settings; which setting this observation came from was not confirmed). — [Hsu et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.12677)

### Inferences
- Inference: the AV-ALOHA recipe (camera DoF appended to the ACT action vector, 50 demos) is the closest published template for a "yaw action" here. The hardware, not the learning, is the blocker: on the wrist link, yaw by forearm roll works only near one spot and otherwise tilts the pusher, so a learned yaw would be coupled to pushing geometry. A hand-mounted (last-joint) camera is the only configuration where a yaw action is a clean extra output.
- Inference: the +-145 degree hand-joint limit plus the measured 45 degree half-angle of usable line visibility covers 190 degrees to each side, i.e. a hairpin (180 degree reversal) is reachable in principle; but the demonstrator must actually turn the camera during demos (ViA/AV-ALOHA used VR headsets; here it would be a second teleop axis), which is a non-trivial addition for two beginners.
- Inference: AV-ALOHA's "all cameras hurt" and "AV hurts when not needed" results mean adding a yaw action to the arc and gentle-meander cases could cost performance; it pays off only on hairpins and self-crossing loops. A reasonable compromise is a fixed yaw strategy learned implicitly (the operator always points the camera toward the upcoming line during demos) rather than a free yaw DoF.

### Gaps
- No active-vision paper used a single wrist camera on a 6-DoF arm as the only sensor with a yaw-only camera action; all had either an extra camera arm or a head.
- No study quantified how many demonstrations are needed to learn camera motion versus arm motion separately.

## Key question 3: Wrist versus third-person cameras in imitation learning; what happens when the target leaves the wrist view

### Takeaway
Wrist (eye-in-hand) views improve generalisation and sample efficiency when the task-relevant object stays in view (Hsu et al.: 69.6 % vs 55.6 % mean in Meta-World, 52 % vs 20 % on a real Franka under shifts; robomimic: removing the wrist view cost 9 % to 43 % relative), but when the target starts or ends outside the hand view, hand-only policies "both train and generalize worse" and need a second view or a wider lens; the 2026 wrist-fisheye study roughly doubled single-wrist-camera success (0.31 to 0.57 average in sim) by widening the field of view from 90 to 235 degrees.

### Cited Findings
- Hsu et al. (ICLR 2022): Meta-World mean success hand+proprioception 69.6 % vs third-person+proprioception 55.6 %; both views + variational information bottleneck 84.4 % vs both unregularised 67.4 % (64 % relative reduction in OOD failure). Real Franka sponge grasp under shifts: hand 52.0 % vs third-person 20.0 % (in-distribution 85 % vs 85 %). Hypothesis: "partial observations can facilitate the acquisition of useful symmetries". — [Hsu et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.12677)
- Hsu et al. on targets outside the hand view: "When hand-centric observability is low, [hand+proprio] both trains and generalizes worse than [third-person+proprio]" in reach-hard and peg-insert-side-hard, where the goal/peg starts left or right of the gripper and "the target box is completely out of view as the agent approaches and grasps the peg". — [Hsu et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.12677)
- robomimic image experiments use one external plus one wrist camera per arm; "removing wrist images results in 9% and 43% relative drops" on Square and Transport; real Can task 73.3 % with wrist vs 43.3 % without, attributed to gripper alignment. — [Mandlekar et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.03298)
- ACT/ALOHA: four 480x640 webcams, two on the follower wrists "allowing for a close-up view of the grippers", one front (rotated 90 degrees), one top; no wrist-camera ablation reported. — [Zhao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.13705)
- AV-ALOHA: wrist-only was the worst setup on Hook Package (8 % vs 44 % static) and Occluded Insertion (15 %). — [AV-ALOHA](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.17435v2)
- Wrist fisheye study (CVPR 2026): real pinhole 60 degrees vs fisheye 180 degrees; sim pinhole 90 degrees vs fisheye 235 degrees. Simulation average success (feature-poor / feature-rich backgrounds): single pinhole 0.31 / 0.34; single fisheye 0.57 / 0.66; double pinhole 0.38 / 0.45; double fisheye 0.72 / 0.75 (200 to 500 demos per task, wrist cameras only, no third view). Fisheye "can overfit more easily to simple scenes"; with 8 diverse real scenes zero-shot success "rapidly exceeds 95%". Recommendations: "Prioritize data collection in visually complex and feature-rich environments", maximise scene diversity, use Random Scale Augmentation U(0.7, 1.3) for cross-lens transfer. — [Rethinking Camera Choice, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02139)
- Practitioner guidance (secondary): LeRobot maintainers recommend "preferably two views", at least 50 episodes with 10 per location, cameras fixed; NVIDIA's SO-101 guide notes the gripper camera "becomes occluded after grasp" and uses a separate front camera. — [Particula blog](https://particula.tech/blog/where-to-put-cameras-robot-imitation-learning); [NVIDIA SO-101 guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/learning/physical-ai/sim-to-real-so-101/latest/08-operating-so101.html)

### Inferences
- Inference: the puck-pushing task is exactly Hsu et al.'s "low hand-centric observability" case whenever the path heads more than 45 degrees off "away from base": the line (the goal) leaves the view, the puck (in view) carries no information about where to go. Published evidence says a hand-only policy will train and generalise worse there; the published remedies are a second (third-person) view with an information bottleneck, or a wider lens. The hard constraint "only the wrist camera" removes the first remedy, which makes the field-of-view and memory questions decisive.
- Inference: the A4 sheet with a black line is a feature-poor scene; the fisheye study warns that wide-FOV policies overfit such scenes, so if a wider lens were used, varying sheet backgrounds, lighting and table texture across the 100 to 300 demos would be necessary.
- Inference: the wrist view's documented strength (gripper-object alignment) is already available here: puck at the bottom of every frame makes the alignment of pusher and puck observable; the weakness (target leaving view) is the whole problem.

### Gaps
- No ablation isolating wrist-camera FOV between 60 and 180 degrees on the same policy; only the 60/90 vs 180/235 pairs exist.
- No study of a single wrist camera as the sole sensor on a pushing (non-grasping) task.

## Key question 4: Design patterns that keep the target in view: reposition-then-push as discrete skills or action modes; multimodal action handling for mode switches

### Takeaway
No imitation-learning paper was found that represents "lift, move around the object, push from a new side" as explicit discrete skills for line/path pushing; the published handles are (a) multimodal action heads (diffusion / flow matching / GMM) that absorb mode switches from demonstrations without labels, and (b) hierarchical BC with a skill selector (BUDS, hierarchical IL) that needs more machinery than two beginners can comfortably add.

### Cited Findings
- Diffusion Policy was built to handle multimodal demonstrations (Push-T benchmark); a follow-up notes Chi et al. found Ta=1 "significantly degrades performance", attributed to "mode switching in multi-modal demonstrations", while on single-modal data Ta=1 did not hurt. — [Chi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04137); [Responsive Noise-Relaying Diffusion Policy, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12724)
- Flow matching for multi-support manipulation (2024): flow matching captures multimodal trajectory distributions and "outperforms diffusion and traditional behavior cloning" in simulation; demonstrated on real whole-body non-prehensile box pushing. — [Flow Matching Imitation Learning for Multi-Support Manipulation](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2407.12381)
- Visual-prompt pushing policy (2026): flow-matching policy producing "reactive, multimodal pushing actions"; argues prior pushing methods "rely on multi-step push plans built from fixed primitives, which limits their versatility". — [Visual Prompt Guided Unified Pushing Policy](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.19193)
- Visual Imitation Made Easy (CoRL 2020): plain BC on 1000 pushing demos with a reacher-grabber; data augmentation (random crops, jitter, cuts, rotations) raised real pushing success from 62.5 % to 87 % on unseen objects. — [Young et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.04899v1)
- BUDS (RA-L 2022): discovers skills from unsegmented demonstrations, trains goal-conditioned skill policies by hierarchical IL and composes them with a meta controller; evaluated in sim and on a real robot. — [Zhu et al. 2022, BUDS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.13841)
- Hierarchical imitation (2018): a high-level policy predicts subgoals and selects a low-level policy. — [Le et al. 2018, Hierarchical Imitation and Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.00590)
- Yu & Altahhan (2025): two-level design where a high-level network picks grasp vs push primitives, but the high level is trained with RL (not pure IL). — [Electronics 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/15/3074)
- robomimic BC-RNN results above show that temporal models help most on the multi-stage Transport task (17.3 % to 71.3 % low-dim PH), which involves handovers i.e. mode changes. — [Mandlekar et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.03298)

### Inferences
- Inference: a hairpin with a 2.4 cm puck on a 1 cm line cannot be pushed through from behind; the pusher must leave contact, circle the puck and re-engage. If the demonstrations contain that behaviour consistently, a multimodal head (Diffusion Policy / ACT with CVAE) is the published way to learn the switch without explicit labels; plain MSE behaviour cloning averages the modes and would hover.
- Inference: a discrete "mode" output (push / reposition) is cheap to add as an extra classification head if the demos are labelled by the teleop interface (e.g. a key press when lifting), and would give the students an interpretable signal; no paper evidence supports or refutes this at 100 to 300 demos, so it is a design choice to test, not a recommendation from the literature.
- Inference: keeping the target in view by repositioning only works if the policy can see the line again after repositioning; with the camera forced to look "away from base", pushing a segment that heads toward the base is impossible to observe from any pusher pose unless the camera is turned, so repositioning alone does not solve the hairpin; it must combine with memory or camera yaw.

### Gaps
- No IL paper found with explicit reposition-then-push skills for pushing along a path; none with labelled action modes in a 100 to 300 demo regime.
- No evidence on how ACT's CVAE versus Diffusion Policy differ on reposition-style mode switches for pushing specifically.

## Key question 5: Mobile-robot line following at hairpins with a forward camera: how loss of the line was handled; imitation-learning results on hairpin or loop tracks

### Takeaway
In mobile-robot line/lane following the loss of the line at sharp turns is handled almost entirely by hand-coded logic (hard-coded turns in an AI Driving Olympics entry; turn-in-place search with relaxed thresholds plus visual-odometry breadcrumbs, 86.6 % recovery in simulation in a 2026 paper; "remember the last direction" heuristics in hobby line followers), and the few imitation-learning line-following papers do not report curve-specific results; the one thesis that tested a context-dependent U-turn with a feed-forward CNN found it "succeeded only occasionally".

### Cited Findings
- AI Driving Olympics 2020 entry (imitation learning, real+sim data): "most Duckietown turns and intersections are standard-shaped, we hard-coded the robot's motion in these situations"; imperfect trajectories were deliberately kept to let the model "perform actions needed to return to the lane". — [Imitation Learning Approach for AIDO, 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.03514)
- Duckietown IL sim-to-real (2022): BC, DAgger and GAIL compared; expert used "different velocity and steering gain values for straights and for corners"; 128 episodes x 768 timesteps (98,304 samples) for BC, plus 65,536 DAgger samples; no curve-vs-straight breakdown or curve failure analysis; without sim-to-real transfer the real robot survived 1 s. — [Imitation Learning for Generalizable Self-driving Policy with Sim-to-real Transfer](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.10797)
- Official Duckietown lane-following docs list known issues: the Duckiebot "cuts white line while driving on inner curves" and interprets intersections as broken lanes with fewer line segments. — [Duckietown lane following docs](https://docs.duckietown.com/daffy/opmanual-duckiebot/demos/supported/lane_following.html)
- Camera-only recovery (2026 preprint, simulation): when the line is lost the robot turns in place while relaxing colour thresholds and waiting for multi-frame confirmation, then uses monocular visual odometry to return to saved breadcrumb positions; successful in 86.6 % of 119 fault-injected episodes, median recovery 3.26 s. — [Self-Healing Visual Recovery for AGVs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.11686)
- Hobby/teaching line followers: memorise the last turn direction and search that way; back up while steering toward the last known direction because driving forward "would carry the robot off the track"; a two-sensor follower on a sharp corner "would turn the wrong direction, thus losing the line". — [FLLCasts lost-state tutorial](https://fllcasts.com/tutorials/215-gapped-crossed-line-following-part-12-escape-the-lost-state); [UNR lab report](https://www.cse.unr.edu/~dbigelow/Archive/Labs/report2.pdf)
- Feed-forward CNN lane following worked indoors, but the U-turn task needing history "succeeded only occasionally". — [NTNU thesis 2018](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2566504)
- A CMU project added an LSTM to an image-to-steering network and reported smooth driving on unseen curvy maps in the Udacity simulator (simulation only). — [CMU RI project page](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/event/learning-based-lane-following-and-changing-behaviors-for-autonomous-vehicle)
- A Duckietown RL+VAE project "was able to learn how to drive in a straight line, but was unable to learn how to drive on curves" (RL, not IL). — [Duckietown project page](https://duckietown.com/?p=107642)

### Inferences
- Inference: the community's answer to "line leaves the forward camera at a hairpin" is a recovery behaviour, not a better single-frame policy. Under an imitation-only constraint the equivalent is to demonstrate the recovery: deliberately include episodes where the demonstrator loses the line and finds it again (the AIDO 2020 team's "keep imperfect trajectories" practice, which is DAgger-like in spirit). Two beginners can do this with teleoperation alone.
- Inference: the "last-seen direction" heuristic is learnable only by a policy with memory (the turn direction is not in the current frame once the line is gone), which ties Question 5 back to Question 1: a memoryless policy can learn at most a fixed search pattern.
- Inference: the mobile-robot case is easier than the arm case in one way (the camera turns with the vehicle, so a turn immediately brings the line back) and this does not hold for the Ned2 with a vertical pusher, where the camera heading is fixed by geometry; so mobile-robot recovery results overestimate what a fixed-heading wrist camera can do.

### Gaps
- No imitation-learning paper or report found that evaluates hairpin or self-crossing loop tracks with a forward camera; Duckietown IL papers aggregate over whole maps.
- No quantitative comparison of memory vs memoryless policies on line loss at sharp turns in any robotics venue.

## Key question 6: Simple engineering options (wider lenses, camera tilt, mirrors, lower mounting, image mirroring augmentation) and their effect on learned policies

### Takeaway
The one well-measured option is a wider lens: a wrist fisheye (180 to 235 degrees) roughly doubled single-camera success and cut localisation error in the 2026 study, at the cost of overfitting to simple scenes unless backgrounds are varied; image mirroring is valid only with a matched action reflection (MirrorDuo for 6-DoF poses, negated steering for cars) and gave no benefit in one driving study; no literature was found on camera tilt, mirrors or lower mounting for learned policies.

### Cited Findings
- Wrist fisheye vs pinhole (CVPR 2026): single-camera sim average 0.31/0.34 (pinhole 90 degrees) vs 0.57/0.66 (fisheye 235 degrees); real pinhole 60 degrees vs fisheye 180 degrees; Pick Cup proprioception-probe error 12.3 cm / 15.3 degrees (pinhole, feature-poor) vs 2.4 cm / 3.4 degrees (fisheye, feature-rich); fisheye policies "can overfit more easily to simple scenes"; naive cross-lens transfer fails from "scale overfitting", fixed by Random Scale Augmentation U(0.7, 1.3). — [Rethinking Camera Choice, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02139)
- Fisheye-VLA (2026): single fixed fisheye camera with pixel resampling to recover acuity instead of synthesising in-hand views. — [Fisheye-VLA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25750)
- MirrorDuo (CoRL 2025): mirrors each demonstration with a reflection matrix on the 6-DoF end-effector pose as augmentation for BC/diffusion; "the mirrored initial pose generally differs from the original due to reflection discontinuities in SO(3)"; gains when demonstrations are spread across both sides of the workspace. — [MirrorDuo](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v305/zhuang25a.html)
- Driving BC: horizontal flip of centre-camera frames with negated steering angle; side cameras excluded because a flip swaps viewpoints. — [Robust Behavioral Cloning for AVs, 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.04767)
- A 2024 driving reality check applied 50 % flips with steering inversion and found augmentation "did not improve its results". — [Behavioral Cloning Models Reality Check, 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.07218)
- Standard image augmentation (random crops, jitter, cuts, rotations) raised real pushing BC from 62.5 % to 87 %. — [Young et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.04899v1)
- Multi-view scaling (2026, secondary via blog): 1 to 5 views raised sim Can 0.18 to 0.37 and Square 0.14 to 0.18; 3 to 5x fewer demos for equal performance. — [Particula blog citing arXiv 2604.00557](https://particula.tech/blog/where-to-put-cameras-robot-imitation-learning)
- Practitioner advice (uncited author practice): measure re-mount tolerance by shifting a camera by 2, 5, 10 cm and 10, 20 degrees yaw with 20 rollouts per step; add the wrist view before adding more episodes. — [Particula blog](https://particula.tech/blog/where-to-put-cameras-robot-imitation-learning)

### Inferences
- Inference: for this task the camera's 9 cm x 7 cm window is the binding limit; a 180 degree lens would make the line visible for nearly all headings except straight back toward the base, which would convert most hairpins and loops from a memory problem into a perception problem. Whether the Niryo Vision Set camera can be swapped or fitted with a wide converter lens is a hardware question outside the literature; the 2026 study's warning about feature-poor scenes applies directly to a plain A4 sheet, so varied backgrounds/lighting in the demos would be mandatory.
- Inference: image mirroring is attractive because the test sheets are left/right symmetric in character (arcs, S-curves), but it is valid only if the Cartesian push step is reflected across the camera's vertical plane consistently and the camera is centred on the pusher axis; if the Vision Set camera is offset from the rod, a mirrored image does not correspond to any real pose (MirrorDuo's SO(3) discontinuity point). Test on held-out demos before relying on it.
- Inference: tilting the camera downward would shrink the look-ahead (already 7 cm) and should be avoided; tilting upward or mounting lower trades look-ahead for width but no measured results exist, and the memory rule "test before hardware changes" applies.

### Gaps
- No published measurements for camera tilt, mirrors or lower mounting on learned manipulation policies.
- No FOV ablation between 60 and 180 degrees; no data on converter lenses on consumer wrist cameras.
- No augmentation study for mirroring in a single wrist-camera pushing setting; the MirrorDuo gains are for third-person + wrist bimanual setups.
