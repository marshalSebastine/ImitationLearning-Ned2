# Demonstration data collection and design for small-data visuomotor imitation learning (puck pushing on Niryo Ned2), as of October 2026

Use-case facts carried into every judgement below: two beginner master's students, deadline 10 January 2027; Niryo Ned2 over pyniryo 1.2.5 (blocking move commands, about one 1 cm tool step per second, no streaming control, "learning mode" lets a person move the arm by hand with motors off); wrist camera 640x480 JPEG at about 9 fps looking straight ahead, seeing a window about 9 cm wide and 7 cm ahead of the puck, puck always at the bottom of the frame; task = push a 2.4 cm red wooden puck (9 g) along a 1 cm black line on an A4 sheet to the line's end; professor's constraint: imitation learning only, "we push the puck ourselves using the robot while recording the video from the webcam mounted onto the end effector; the video stream will be the model's input and the robot movement the model's output"; evaluation on an unseen path sheet; six example sheets; feasible budget roughly 100 to 300 episodes of 25 to 40 steps (about 2,500 to 12,000 image-action pairs).

Candidate methods: (a) keyboard-driven stepping, (b) hand-guiding in learning mode with joint and image logging, (c) scripted privileged teacher that knows the line coordinates, (d) DAgger-style corrections after a policy fails.

Age marking: results from 2011 to 2021 are marked "(older)"; 2023 to 2026 results are current.

## Key question 1: Teleoperation vs kinesthetic (hand-guiding) vs scripted or privileged-expert demonstrations

### Takeaway
The only direct head-to-head on real manipulation (Li, Cui and Sadigh, 2025) found kinesthetic demonstrations gave the cleanest actions and the best diffusion-policy models on two of three tasks, but only because the recorded poses were replayed by the robot to re-capture images without the human's hand; mixing a few kinesthetic with teleoperated demos beat either alone by about 20 percent. Privileged-teacher pipelines (Learning by Cheating, older, driving sim) show that a scripted expert that can be queried at any state turns a 20 percent camera-only policy into a 100 percent one, but method (c) conflicts with the professor's literal wording and must be cleared with him first.

### Cited Findings
- Li, Cui, Sadigh (2025), "How to Train Your Robots? The Impact of Demonstration Modality on Imitation Learning": Franka Panda, three tasks (Open Drawer, Flip Glass, Push Sanitizer), one expert gave 100 trajectories per modality per task, diffusion policy, 20 evaluation trials per model (half in-distribution, half out-of-distribution). Kinesthetic data gave the best models for Open Drawer (large margin) and Flip Glass, and underperformed on the high-contact-force Push Sanitizer task. Kinesthetic had lower action variance and lower jerkiness except on Push Sanitizer; teleoperation gave higher state diversity. Mixing kinesthetic with VR data improved the best models by about 20 percent on average over single-modality data; for Flip Glass a 100-demo mix reached 75 percent success, 5 points above the best all-kinesthetic model. VR controller vs SpaceMouse showed no statistically significant difference. — [arXiv 2503.07017](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07017v1)
- Same paper, protocol detail that matters for a wrist camera: kinesthetic poses were recorded, then replayed on the robot so that the visual observations are "clean ... unimpeded by the demonstrator's hand". Replay roughly doubled collection time for the contact-heavy task, and replay failed on first try for Push Sanitizer because replayed poses did not reproduce the demonstrated contact forces (no force sensor; a heuristic correction term produced jerky actions). — [arXiv 2503.07017](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07017v1)
- Same paper, user study: 12 students, 5 successful demos per modality each. Kinesthetic needed less practice time (p = 0.038), had lower mental demand and frustration and higher perceived performance (p much less than 0.01), but the highest physical demand (p = 0.016). Most participants preferred kinesthetic for controlling the robot but chose teleoperation for large-scale collection. Non-expert data was noisy with high cross-subject variance. — [arXiv 2503.07017](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07017v1)
- KineDex (2025, dexterous hand, single framework): kinesthetic collection roughly doubled collection speed and reached near 100 percent task success versus under 50 percent for teleoperation in that setup. — [arXiv 2505.01974](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01974v1)
- A 2022 paper on force-feedback demonstrations notes that kinesthetic teaching "introduces visual distraction from the human's presence in the scene". — [arXiv 2202.09574](https://arxiv.org/html/2202.09574v2)
- Isaac Lab teleoperation and imitation guidance (NVIDIA docs): "Better devices yield better data", SpaceMouse is preferred over keyboard; "Longer time horizon is harder to learn for a policy"; "Do not pause. Perform smooth, continuous motions instead"; "Take a direct path"; "Action noise makes policies more robust". — [Isaac Lab docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/v2.0.2/source/overview/teleop_imitation.html)
- robomimic (Mandlekar et al., CoRL 2021, older): image-based BC-RNN success, Proficient-Human (PH, 200 demos from one operator) vs Multi-Human (MH, 300 demos from six operators): Lift 100 vs 100, Can 98.0 vs 96.0, Square 82.0 vs 76.7, Transport 72.0 vs 42.0. Average demo length Square 151 steps (PH) vs 269 (MH), Transport 469 vs 653; the authors attribute lower MH results to "suboptimal and multimodal data". Demos were collected with RoboTurk smartphone teleoperation at about 20 Hz. — [arXiv 2108.03298](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.03298v2)
- robomimic "Machine-Generated" (MG) data, which came from reinforcement-learning agent checkpoints of mixed quality rather than a clean scripted expert: BC-RNN reached only 70.7 (Lift) and 68.7 (Can) on MG versus 100 and 100 on PH; BCQ was strongest on MG (91.3 Lift, 75.3 Can) but collapsed on human data (7.3 on Transport PH). — [arXiv 2108.03298](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.03298v2)
- Learning by Cheating (Chen et al., CoRL 2019, older, CARLA driving sim): a privileged teacher trained on 100 expert trajectories at 10 fps (about 157K frames, about 4 hours) supervised a camera-only student. On the CoRL2017 navigation benchmark (test town and weather), direct one-stage camera imitation reached 20 percent success, a vanilla two-stage pipeline 16 percent, and the full method with white-box teacher and on-policy (DAgger-style) supervision 100 percent; on NoCrash dense traffic 85 percent vs 67 percent for the camera-only CILRS baseline. The teacher "can be queried in any state the student reaches ... so no human expert is needed". The authors say direct camera imitation "combines two hard problems, learning to see and learning to act, into one learning task". — [ar5iv 1912.12294](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.12294)
- Privileged teacher-student distillation in manipulation and humanoids (2024 to 2026) uses the same recipe (teacher on privileged state, vision student trained with DAgger labels); one 2025 paper warns that querying a privileged teacher at arbitrary student states "leading to a dataset that becomes increasingly unrealizable, causing policy divergence" and proposes querying only at critical states. — [arXiv 2505.09546](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09546v1); [VIRAL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.15200)
- MimicGen-style scripted generation from a few human demos: Isaac Lab notes roughly 10 successful human demos are enough to generate 1,000 trials; a 2025 paper citing Mandlekar et al. (2023) reports policy performance on generated data "can be comparable to performance on an equal amount of human demonstrations". — [Isaac Lab docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/v2.0.2/source/overview/teleop_imitation.html); [arXiv 2504.13803](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.13803)
- Belkhale, Cui, Sadigh (NeurIPS 2023), "Data Quality in Imitation Learning": on robomimic Square with BC, success was 58 percent (proficient human), 36 percent (Better operators), 12 percent (Okay), 2 percent (Worse); horizon length rose from 150 to 350 steps across these groups; "state diversity was not correlated with performance"; recommendation: "Curate demonstrations toward more consistent actions, i.e., lower expert action entropy". — [ar5iv 2306.02437](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.02437)
- Teleoperation interface comparison (Jan 2026, 25 participants): VR controller beat SpaceMouse on success rate (especially dynamic tasks), execution time, workload and usability; no phone or keyboard condition. — [arXiv 2601.13042](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13042v1)

### Inferences
- (Inference) Distribution match with test-time observations is best for method (a) keyboard stepping and for a scripted teacher executed through the same stepping code: at test time the loop will also be "capture a still frame, move 1 cm (blocking), capture again", so the training images are literally the test-time images (static arm, no motion blur, no hand). Hand-guiding (b) produces continuous, blurred frames with the operator's hand or arm in the wrist camera's forward view; the Li et al. fix (record joints, replay with motors on, re-capture images) would be mandatory, and because pyniryo moves block, the replay would itself be a sequence of 1 cm step moves, so (b) collapses into (a) plus extra time (Li et al. saw replay double collection time on one task).
- (Inference) The usual penalty on keyboard teleoperation (jerky, axis-aligned motion, Isaac Lab) does not apply in the same way here, because the action space is already a 1 cm step and the arm settles between steps; what remains is the risk of inconsistent operator decisions (Belkhale: action consistency matters more than state diversity), which a written stepping rule for both students can control.
- (Inference) The robomimic MG result should not be read as "scripted demos are bad": MG data was mixed-quality RL rollouts. A deterministic scripted teacher on this task would have zero cross-demo action divergence, the property Belkhale et al. identify as the main quality lever, and it can label any state the policy reaches (Learning by Cheating), which no human method can do at scale.
- (Inference, conflict flag) Method (c) does not match the professor's wording "we push the puck ourselves using the robot". It should be presented to him as an optional labelling aid (for example, a teacher used only to label recovery steps), not as the primary demonstration source, unless he approves. Methods (a), (b) and (d) are all literally "pushing the puck ourselves using the robot".
- (Inference) Li et al.'s replay failure was force-related (sanitiser bottle); a 9 g puck pushed by a rod is a low-force task, so replay fidelity is unlikely to be the problem; the hand-in-view problem is.

### Gaps
- No study found that compares keyboard stepping against hand-guiding on a low-cost arm with a wrist camera; the modality paper used VR, SpaceMouse and kinesthetic on a Franka.
- Li et al. report per-modality policy success rates only in figures; the text gives the direction and the about-20-percent mixing gain, not the full table.
- No source found that measures how often a hand-guiding operator's hand appears in a wrist camera view, or quantifies the image-domain gap this creates.

## Key question 2: DAgger, HG-DAgger, DART, EnsembleDAgger, ThriftyDAgger and simple recovery-data practices

### Takeaway
Across older driving work and 2025 real-robot work, adding on-policy corrections is the single most reliable way to raise closed-loop success (HG-DAgger: 0 collisions and 0 road departures vs 11 and 66 for BC in the first 5,000 on-vehicle samples; RaC 2025: about 10x less collection time than prior methods), and human-gated intervention (the human takes over and only those steps are labelled) is both the simplest and the best-behaved protocol; LeRobot now ships it as a built-in rollout mode. For a 1 cm-per-second blocking arm, the cheapest form is "policy proposes a step, human approves or overrides with a key", which is HG-DAgger with no safety issue.

### Cited Findings
- DART (Laskey et al., CoRL 2017, older): off-policy alternative to DAgger; Gaussian noise is added to the supervisor's actions during demonstration so the dataset contains recoveries around the expert's own path; the noise level is optimised iteratively to approximate the trained robot's error. On MuJoCo Humanoid, DART was up to 3x faster in computation and reduced the supervisor's cumulative reward by only 5 percent during collection, whereas DAgger executed policies with 80 percent less reward than the supervisor. Physical grasping-in-clutter on a Toyota HSR with four human subjects: on average a 62 percent performance increase over behaviour cloning. — [arXiv 1703.09327](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/1703.09327); [IEOR Berkeley summary](https://ieor.berkeley.edu/?p=15613)
- DART practical guidance (BAIR blog): keep the noise small ("supervisors can usually handle small random disturbances around their current action"); inject noise in real time during teleoperation rather than asking humans for retroactive labels, which the authors found hard for humans in their DAgger study; the training target is the deterministic supervisor action (clean label; stated as an inference by the fetched summary). — [BAIR blog, DART](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2017/10/26/dart/)
- HG-DAgger (Kelly et al., 2018/2019, older, driving): the human is the gate; the novice acts until the expert sees it enter an unsafe region, takes over, returns it to safety, hands back control; "Expert action labels are only collected and added to D during these recovery trajectories" (novice-controlled steps are not labelled). On the test vehicle, in the first 5,000 samples: BC 11 collisions and 66 road departures; DAgger 11 and 11; HG-DAgger 0 and 0; HG-DAgger steering distribution was 21.1 percent closer to human data than DAgger's. Initialisation was BC on 10,000 expert labels, then five epochs of 2,000 labels each. The authors argue classic DAgger mixing degrades human labels because the human is not in control ("perceived actuator lag") and that "higher quality action labels can be acquired when the human expert is given stretches of uninterrupted control authority". — [ar5iv 1810.02890](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.02890)
- EnsembleDAgger (Menda et al., 2018/IROS 2019, older): ensemble variance as a "doubt" measure that decides when the novice may act; HG-DAgger notes it "does not provide a means of selecting that threshold"; results reported only on inverted pendulum and HalfCheetah, no numbers found. — [arXiv 1807.08364](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.08364); [ar5iv 1810.02890](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.02890)
- ThriftyDAgger (Hoque et al., CoRL 2021, older): intervention criterion balancing task performance and supervisor burden; user study (N = 10) reported human and robot performance increases of 58 percent and 80 percent over the next-best algorithm with 21 percent fewer interventions; with interventions at execution time it reached 100 percent success on the simulated and physical cable-routing task. — [arXiv 2109.08273](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.08273); [UT Austin slides](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~robertom/cs391r_fall2022/slides/HG-DAgger.pdf)
- BC-Z (2022, older): HG-DAgger "significantly improves task performance over cloning expert demonstrations" on a manipulation task (secondary ablation). — [arXiv 2202.02005](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.02005)
- RaC (Hu et al., 2025): after imitation pretraining, operators intervene when a rollout is about to fail, rewind the robot to a familiar state ("recovery") and demonstrate the right behaviour ("correction"); the policy is fine-tuned on these; beats prior state of the art on three real bimanual tasks and one simulated assembly task with about 10x less data-collection time; "performance scales linearly with the number of recovery maneuvers the policy performs at test time". — [arXiv 2509.07953](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07953)
- LeRobot human-in-the-loop collection (2026 docs): `lerobot-rollout --strategy.type=dagger`; loop is watch, pause (no frames recorded during pause), take over (recovery then correction, all movements recorded), return control, repeat within the episode; both autonomous and human segments are recorded; fine-tune on the combined dataset and iterate "deploy, collect, fine-tune, repeat". Intervene on "irreversible mistake", "hesitating or showing uncertain behavior", "deviating from the expected trajectory"; corrections should be "confident, clean", "Don't overcorrect". Example command uses 50 HIL episodes. The docs state this builds on DAgger, HG-DAgger, RaC and Physical Intelligence's RECAP. — [LeRobot HIL docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/hil_data_collection)
- Belkhale et al. (2023): distribution shift is bounded by action divergence weighted by time-to-go, "so early-step mismatches have an outsized effect"; adding system (transition) noise during collection generally helped up to a point (Square, 200 episodes: 55.7 percent at sigma = 0.05 vs 82.0 percent at sigma = 0.2), whereas adding expert action noise "often hurt, especially with small datasets". — [ar5iv 2306.02437](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.02437)
- Isaac Lab: "Action noise makes policies more robust." — [Isaac Lab docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/v2.0.2/source/overview/teleop_imitation.html)

### Inferences
- (Inference) Simplest two-person protocol consistent with the sources: Round 0, collect keyboard-stepped demos; Round 1 onward, run the trained policy one step at a time; the operator sees the proposed step on screen and either presses "accept" (robot executes the policy's step, frame stored unlabelled or labelled "policy") or presses the correct direction (robot executes the human step, frame stored with the human label). This is HG-DAgger (human-gated, only human steps labelled) with the keyboard as the gate; because each step is 1 cm and blocking, there is no actuator-lag or safety problem that motivated the gating in driving. Retrain on demos plus corrections, repeat 2 to 3 rounds. HG-DAgger's own schedule (10,000 initial labels then 2,000 per round) scaled to this budget suggests about 100 to 150 initial episodes, then 20 to 40 correction episodes per round.
- (Inference) A DART analogue for a discrete-step robot: with probability p (for example 0.15 to 0.25 per step), the collection script executes a random 1 cm lateral or diagonal step instead of the operator's key, then the operator corrects; the stored label for the perturbed frame is the operator's intended (clean) step, and the frames after the perturbation contain the recovery. This matches DART's "small noise, real-time correction" guidance and Belkhale's finding that system noise helps while action-label noise hurts. The magnitude (p) is a guess and should be tuned on the first 20 episodes.
- (Inference, conflict flag) In DAgger rounds, the robot executes policy actions for accepted steps; the human still "pushes the puck using the robot" in the corrections, and the labels used for training come from the humans, so this stays within "imitation learning only". The autonomous segments should still be described openly to the professor.
- (Inference) The scripted teacher (c), if allowed even as an auxiliary, is the only way to label every state the policy reaches without a human in the loop (Learning by Cheating); but 2025 work warns against querying it at states far from anything realisable, so limit it to states near the line.

### Gaps
- No real-robot study found that reports closed-loop success before and after a small number (20 to 50) of human-gated correction episodes on a single-object pushing task; the HG-DAgger and RaC numbers are for driving and bimanual tasks.
- EnsembleDAgger and ThriftyDAgger numbers against plain BC on manipulation were not found; the ThriftyDAgger gains are relative to "the next best algorithm".
- DART's exact noise magnitude for human teleoperation is given only via its optimisation procedure; no simple rule of thumb was found.

## Key question 3: How many demonstrations are enough

### Takeaway
For single-task, single-scene manipulation, 50 demos per "environment-object pair" is the empirically recommended unit (Lin et al., ICLR 2025, 50 demos per pair, about 32 pairs for about 90 percent success in unseen rooms), 50 episodes is the LeRobot default and the ACT/ALOHA baseline (64 to 96 percent on four tasks), and robomimic shows easy tasks saturate at 20 to 50 percent of a 200-demo set while harder ones keep improving to 200. The 100 to 300 episode budget here is adequate if it is spread over many paths rather than repeated on the six sheets.

### Cited Findings
- Lin et al. (ICLR 2025 oral), "Data Scaling Laws in Imitation Learning for Robotic Manipulation": over 40,000 UMI demonstrations and over 15,000 real rollouts with Diffusion Policy; generalisation follows an approximate power law in the number of environments and objects; with 8, 16 and 32 environment-object pairs, performance plateaus at 400, 800 and 1,600 demonstrations respectively (50 per pair); "Recommended: 50 demonstrations per environment-object pair for tasks of similar difficulty. More demanding dexterous tasks may need more." With 32 objects, "using 12.5 percent of demonstrations nearly matches 100 percent". Four collectors in one afternoon (32 pairs x 50 demos) produced policies with 85.0 percent (Pour Water), 92.5 percent (Mouse Arrangement), 87.5 percent (Fold Towels) and 90.0 percent (Unplug Charger) success in 8 unseen environments. — [arXiv 2410.18647](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18647v3)
- robomimic (2021, older) dataset-size ablation, image observations, Proficient-Human (20 / 50 / 100 percent of 200 demos, i.e. 40 / 100 / 200): Lift 100 / 100 / 100; Can 83.3 / 97.3 / 98.0; Square 29.3 / 64.7 / 82.0; Transport 30.7 / 60.1 / 72.0. Multi-Human (of 300): Square 27.3 / 50.7 / 76.7; Transport 8.0 / 25.3 / 42.0. Text: Lift and Can reach 75 to 100 percent with 20 percent of the data; Square and Transport "suffer substantially at 50 percent or 20 percent". — [arXiv 2108.03298](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.03298v2)
- ACT / ALOHA (2023): 50 human demonstrations per task, object position randomised along a 15 cm range, 50 Hz; success 96, 84, 64 and 92 percent on four tasks. — [HF forum summary citing ACT](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/what-is-the-expected-success-rate-for-a-act-trained-model-using-50-episodes/178517); [claru.ai ACT summary](https://claru.ai/models/act-aloha)
- ALOHA Unleashed (2024): training on 100 / 75 / 50 / 25 percent of the data gave similar results on the easier shirt task but clearly worse results on ShirtMessy. — [arXiv 2410.13126](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.13126v1)
- Diffusion Policy (2023): real-world Push-T (push a T block to a target with a stick, then move to an end zone; the closest published analogue to pushing a puck) used 136 proficient-human demonstrations recorded with a 3Dconnexion SpaceMouse at 10 Hz on a UR5; the operator "had practiced for many hours beforehand"; initial T pose randomised by pushing or tossing it onto the table; success 95 percent (Diffusion Policy, end-to-end CNN) vs 0 percent (IBC) and 20 percent (LSTM-GMM) in 20 trials; simulated Push-T used 200 demos. Real-robot settings: observation horizon To = 2, action horizon Ta = 6, prediction horizon Tp = 16; 10 Hz policy commands linearly interpolated to 125 Hz. — [arXiv 2303.04137](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.04137v5)
- InterACT (2024): in that paper's nine bimanual tasks, "with only 50 demonstrations, the Diffusion policy failed to execute any" task, untuned. — [arXiv 2409.07914](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.07914)
- LeRobot imitation-learning tutorial (2026 docs): "We suggest recording at least 50 episodes, with 10 episodes per location. Keep the cameras fixed and maintain consistent grasping behavior throughout the recordings ... A good rule of thumb is you should be able to do the task yourself by only looking at the camera images." Defaults: `num_episodes=50`, `episode_time_s=60`, `reset_time_s=60`. "Avoid adding too much variation too quickly, as it may hinder your results." — [LeRobot il_robots docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/il_robots)
- Community evidence (HF forum, 2025/2026, non-staff): an SO-ARM101 experiment with 60 demonstrations gave 60 percent strict success "with failures clustered in sparsely demonstrated areas"; a LeRobot issue reported 66 percent for ACT on ALOHA v0.6.1; "more episodes on nearly the same paths did not fix unseen-position failures". — [HF forum thread](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/what-is-the-expected-success-rate-for-a-act-trained-model-using-50-episodes/178517)
- SEIL (equivariant model plus simulated transitions): learns "non-trivial manipulation tasks within ten demonstrations" in simulation, and argues image-level augmentation alone is insufficient at that scale. — [SEIL via NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10487652)

### Inferences
- (Inference) Mapping the scaling-law vocabulary onto this task: the "object" is fixed (one puck), and the "environment" is the local line geometry visible in the 9 x 7 cm window plus the puck's offset from the line. A 300-episode budget at 50 per "environment" covers only six geometries (the six sheets); the scaling-law paper's advice is to trade repeats for diversity, so 20 to 30 distinct path sheets with 10 to 15 episodes each (varying start point and lateral offset) is the better use of the same 300 episodes.
- (Inference) In action complexity the task is nearer robomimic Lift or Can (one object, short horizon, few decisions per step) than Square or Transport; those saturated with 40 to 100 demos. The generalisation demand (unseen sheet) is what pushes the number up, not the motor skill.
- (Inference) 300 episodes x 25 to 40 steps gives 7,500 to 12,000 frames, roughly the frame count of robomimic Lift PH (200 x about 50) or real Push-T (136 demos at 10 Hz); small CNN policies trained fine at that scale in those papers.

### Gaps
- No published success-vs-number-of-demos curve exists for line-following pushing with a wrist camera; the closest analogues (Push-T, Lift) differ in camera (external vs wrist) and goal (fixed target vs path).
- The LeRobot "50 episodes" figure is a tutorial default, not a benchmarked threshold; the HF forum answer explicitly says a 50-episode success benchmark "doesn't exist".

## Key question 4: Diversity for generalisation to unseen paths

### Takeaway
The strongest 2025 evidence says variety of environments beats repeats ("Diversity is all you need": adding environments helps even with total demos held constant), while 2023 evidence warns that state diversity bought at the price of inconsistent actions hurts; LeRobot's practical advice is 10 episodes per location, varied gradually, with fixed cameras and stable lighting. For an unseen path, the policy needs every local line shape and many puck-to-line offsets, not many repeats of the same six sheets.

### Cited Findings
- Lin et al. (2025): "Diversity matters more than raw demonstration count"; "Adding environments helps even when total demonstrations are held constant"; environment generalisation is harder than object generalisation; with 8 training objects normalised scores exceed 0.8, with 32 objects 0.9; multiple objects per environment help only when there are few environments (at 16 environments the gain is negligible); recommended strategy "collect data in as many diverse environments as possible, with only one unique object in each environment". Collection protocol: "randomize the gripper's initial height and orientation, and spread object positions as widely as the robot's kinematic and dynamic limits allow". — [arXiv 2410.18647](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18647v3)
- Belkhale et al. (2023): "state diversity is not always beneficial. Coverage gained at the cost of action consistency can perform worse than less coverage with more consistent actions"; in the human datasets "state diversity was not correlated with performance". — [ar5iv 2306.02437](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.02437)
- LeRobot il_robots (2026): "at least 50 episodes, with 10 episodes per location"; "After achieving reliable grasping performance, you can start introducing more variations ... additional grasp locations, different grasping techniques, and altering camera positions. Avoid adding too much variation too quickly." — [LeRobot il_robots docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/il_robots)
- Hugging Face "What makes a good dataset" checklist (lerobot-datasets blog): "Maintain neutral, stable lighting (avoid overly yellow or blue tones)"; "Use a static, non-distracting background, or apply controlled variations"; "The only moving objects should be the follower arm and the manipulated items (avoid human limbs/bodies)"; "Use preferably two camera views"; "Record in high resolution (at least 480x640 / 720p)". — [HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/lerobot-datasets)
- ACT/ALOHA (2023): object position randomised over a 15 cm range during collection. — [HF forum summary](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/what-is-the-expected-success-rate-for-a-act-trained-model-using-50-episodes/178517)
- Diffusion Policy real Push-T (2023): initial T pose randomised by pushing or tossing the block. — [arXiv 2303.04137](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.04137v5)
- Community: failures "clustered in sparsely demonstrated areas"; "coverage of the workspace matters more than raw count". — [HF forum thread](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/what-is-the-expected-success-rate-for-a-act-trained-model-using-50-episodes/178517)
- Third-party tutorial (Robotics Center): reset the scene "to a slightly different initial setup" between episodes (object placement, lighting angle), described as "the single biggest factor in how well a trained policy generalizes". — [roboticscenter.ai tutorial](https://www.roboticscenter.ai/tutorials/record-lerobot-dataset/)
- Vendor guide (Trossen Robotics): a dataset recorded under one narrow set of conditions "can teach a policy to replay one trajectory rather than the underlying behavior"; vary object placement, lighting and recovery behaviour; validate the first samples with the dataset visualiser and remove failed episodes. — [Trossen blog](https://www.trossenrobotics.com/post/robot-data-collection-best-practices-imitation-learning); [Trossen dataset guide](https://www.trossenrobotics.com/post/lerobot-dataset-guide)
- MirrorDuo (2026): mirrored demonstration pairs as augmentation; gains "largest when demonstrations cover both sides of the workspace"; mirrored-workspace transfer with 0 to 5 target demos. — [arXiv 2606.20048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.20048)

### Inferences
- (Inference) Because the camera sees only a 9 x 7 cm window, the "environment" for this policy is the local line segment, not the whole sheet; a hairpin, a crossing and a gentle arc are different environments even on the same sheet. The six sheets contain many local shapes, but curvature radii and crossing angles should be deliberately varied by printing extra sheets (cheap) so the unseen test sheet's local shapes are inside the training distribution.
- (Inference) Starting positions: start episodes at several points along each line, with the puck deliberately offset laterally (about 0.5 to 1.0 cm, i.e. up to one puck radius) and rotated, so the dataset contains recovery states; this is the Lin et al. "randomise initial pose" and ACT "15 cm randomisation" practice scaled to the task, and it is the cheapest substitute for DAgger data.
- (Inference) Lighting: keep it stable during the first dataset (HF checklist) and vary it moderately only once the policy works on seen sheets (LeRobot "avoid too much variation too quickly"); use colour-jitter augmentation (Q7) for the rest. Crossings are a special case: with a forward-looking camera the policy must choose the straight-through branch; include enough crossing episodes that this choice is consistent in the data (Belkhale: consistency over coverage).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on how many distinct paths or layouts are needed for a line-following policy to generalise to a new path; the environment counts in Lin et al. (8 to 32 rooms) are the nearest quantitative anchor and refer to whole scenes, not path geometries.
- No quantitative evidence found on lighting variation for wrist-camera policies beyond the HF checklist's "stable lighting" advice.

## Key question 5: Demonstration quality (consistency, proficient vs mixed, smoothness, filtering and weighting)

### Takeaway
Operator consistency is the dominant quality factor: on robomimic Square, success fell from 58 to 36 to 12 to 2 percent as operator skill fell from proficient to Better, Okay and Worse (Belkhale et al. 2023), while robomimic itself found that adding weaker operators' demos still helped a history-aware BC-RNN (Square 78 with all MH vs 66 with Better only). So: agree on one stepping rule, keep demos short and direct, discard failures, but do not throw away merely imperfect successes; at 100 to 300 episodes, hard filtering by a simple rule beats learned weighting.

### Cited Findings
- robomimic (2021, older): BC-RNN on all MH data beat the "Better"-operators-only subset: Can 100.0 vs 99.3, Square 78.0 vs 66.0; adding "Worse" demos was "uniformly" helpful for BC-RNN while most other algorithms declined; BC-RNN beat plain BC by 7 to 35 percent across datasets, and the authors call history dependence "crucial for good performance"; RNN sequence lengths of 10, 30 and 50 did not differ significantly. — [arXiv 2108.03298](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.03298v2)
- robomimic: demo lengths Square 151 (PH) vs 269 (MH) steps, Transport 469 vs 653; "Less proficient operators take longer". — [arXiv 2108.03298](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.03298v2)
- Belkhale et al. (2023): Square success 58 percent (PH), 36 (Better), 12 (Okay), 2 (Worse), horizon 150 to 350 steps; "Action variance did not track success cleanly"; recommendations: curate toward consistent actions (lower expert action entropy), encourage transition diversity while keeping coverage dense, "Treat horizon length as a rough proxy, not a direct target". — [ar5iv 2306.02437](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.02437)
- Li et al. (2025): kinesthetic demos had lower action variance and jerkiness (second derivative of actions) and produced the best models on two of three tasks; mixing modalities gave about 20 percent. — [arXiv 2503.07017](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07017v1)
- Isaac Lab: "Keep demonstrations short. Shorter demonstrations mean fewer decisions for the policy"; "Take a direct path"; "Do not pause"; irregular motion "is hard for a policy to decipher"; press R to discard a flawed demo; "collect more human demos than needed and keep the ones that succeed". — [Isaac Lab docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/v2.0.2/source/overview/teleop_imitation.html)
- Diffusion Policy (2023): idle (pause) actions were not removed from the real Push-T training data; the authors say this "likely contributed to LSTM-GMM and IBC overfitting small actions and getting stuck", while Diffusion Policy coped. — [arXiv 2303.04137](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.04137v5)
- DemInf (Hejna et al., RSS 2025): scores each trajectory by its contribution to the mutual information between states and actions (k-NN estimator on VAE embeddings); filtering with it gave a 5 to 10 percent improvement on robomimic and improvements on real ALOHA and Franka setups. — [arXiv 2502.08623](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08623v2)
- Demo-SCORE (2025) hard-filters demonstrations using rollout success signals; a student report found "gains are primarily driven by thresholding rather than soft reweighting". — [CS224R report](https://cs224r.stanford.edu/projects/pdfs/Sydney%20Yan%20Tracy%20Y%20Wei%20Evy%20Zhu%20Shen%20submission_416285569/CS224R_Final_Project_Report-2.pdf)
- LeRobot: Left arrow or `r` cancels and re-records the current episode; "maintain consistent grasping behavior throughout the recordings". — [LeRobot il_robots docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/il_robots)
- Vendor guidance: "a smaller, well-curated dataset can beat a larger set of noisy demonstrations"; flag and remove failed episodes. — [Trossen blog](https://www.trossenrobotics.com/post/robot-data-collection-best-practices-imitation-learning)

### Inferences
- (Inference) With a fixed 1 cm step, "smoothness" and "no pauses" translate into: no back-and-forth steps, no idle steps (a repeated frame with a zero action), and a fixed rule for when to turn (for example, step along the line's local direction; if the puck centre is more than half a line width off, take one diagonal step toward the line). Both students should follow the same written rule so their data looks like one proficient operator (robomimic PH) rather than two operators of different style (MH).
- (Inference) Filtering rule for this budget: keep an episode if the puck reached the end marker, the step count is within about 1.3x the shortest successful episode on that path (Belkhale's horizon proxy), and no step reversed the previous one; put failed or aborted episodes in a separate folder (they are useful later as recovery data if the failure segment is cut). Learned weighting (DemInf) is not worth implementing for a two-person course project; its gain was 5 to 10 percent on much larger sets.
- (Inference) Pauses are a real risk in keyboard stepping if the operator hesitates and the script logs a frame with a "no move" action; the script should log a frame only when a move is issued, except for the deliberate final "stop" step (Q6).

### Gaps
- No source quantifies the effect of mixing two operators who follow the same written rule; robomimic's MH operators were uninstructed.
- No real-robot evidence found for weighting (rather than filtering) demos at the 100 to 300 episode scale.

## Key question 6: Data logging hygiene (synchronisation, stale frames, stop labelling, episode boundaries, metadata)

### Takeaway
Current practice (UMI 2024, HiFi-UMI 2026, LeRobot v3) is to timestamp every stream, align observations to the slowest stream (the camera) and store raw frames with per-frame episode and frame indices in a standard format; for a blocking-step robot the natural fix for stale frames is to capture the frame only after the move has returned and the camera buffer has been flushed, and to log one explicit "stop" step at the end of each episode.

### Cited Findings
- LeRobotDataset v3 (2026): tabular data (states, actions, timestamps) in Parquet, camera frames in MP4 shards; `meta/info.json` holds the feature schema and fps, `meta/stats.json` normalisation statistics, `meta/tasks.jsonl` task descriptions, `meta/episodes/` per-episode lengths and offsets; per-frame fields include `observation.state`, `action`, `observation.images.<camera>`, `timestamp`; episode boundaries are "resolved through metadata, not filenames"; `dataset.finalize()` must be called before use or the Parquet files are corrupt; image transforms are "applied during training time only, not during recording", so raw images are stored. — [LeRobot dataset v3 docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/lerobot-dataset-v3)
- LeRobot recording loop (2026): `episode_time_s` and `reset_time_s` phases; keys: Right arrow or `n` ends the episode early, Left arrow or `r` cancels and re-records, `q` stops and encodes; checkpointing and `--resume=true`; `lerobot-replay` replays recorded actions to "test the repeatability of your robot's actions". — [LeRobot il_robots docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/il_robots)
- HF dataset checklist: record at about 30 fps; consistent, interpretable feature names (for example `images.wrist.left`, not `images.phone`); task description 25 to 50 characters; avoid episodes "with only 1 or very few frames"; if deleting episodes "make sure to update the metadata files accordingly". — [HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/lerobot-datasets)
- UMI (2024): policy interfaces "assume synchronized observation streams and immediate action execution, physical robot systems do not conform to this assumption"; fix: "align all observations with respect to the stream with the highest latency (usually the camera)", use each image's capture timestamp to interpolate proprioception, and send commands ahead of time to compensate execution latency. — [arXiv 2402.10329](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.10329)
- HiFi-UMI (2026): common observation time t_obs = t_now minus the largest calibrated sensor latency; latency-compensated command buffer. — [arXiv 2607.25895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25895)
- DexUMI (2025): camera latency measured by filming a rolling QR code of the computer's system time; joint data interpolated onto camera timestamps. — [arXiv 2505.21864](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.21864)
- Diffusion Policy (2023): real policy at 10 Hz interpolated to 125 Hz; the simulated latency ablation reported performance holding up to about 4 steps of latency (as summarised from the paper text). — [arXiv 2303.04137](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.04137v5)
- robomimic real-robot logging (2021, older): demos at about 20 Hz; 640x480 frames centre-cropped to 480x480 and resized to 120x120 (240x240 for Tool Hang). — [arXiv 2108.03298](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.03298v2)
- Isaac Lab: "Leave a buffer at the end of recordings, since recording that stops exactly when success triggers may not re-trigger on replay". — [Isaac Lab docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/v2.0.2/source/overview/teleop_imitation.html)
- Learned stopping (2020, adversarial IL, older): give the agent a special "Done" action and pad every expert demonstration with it as the last action "just before the episode terminates"; reported to speed training. — [arXiv 2002.00412](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.00412)
- Community timing warning: "Record loop is running slower (5.6 Hz) than the target FPS (30.0 Hz)"; advice to check camera FPS and USB transport, compare teleop vs rollout timing on the same hardware, and inspect recorded episodes for timing problems. — [HF forum thread](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/what-is-the-expected-success-rate-for-a-act-trained-model-using-50-episodes/178517)
- Student write-up on a low-cost arm with LeRobot v3: imitation learning "is hurt by noisy timing labels or inconsistent logs". — [Medium post](https://medium.com/@hehu2280/behind-a-failed-robot-learning-project-dataset-engineering-training-and-inference-efc1c2bd10e8)

### Inferences
- (Inference) For pyniryo's blocking moves the collection loop should be: (1) move returns, (2) wait a short settle time, (3) drain the camera stream and keep the newest frame whose timestamp is after the move-complete time (the 9 fps JPEG stream will otherwise hand back a frame from during the motion), (4) record frame plus robot state, (5) the operator or policy chooses the action, (6) log the action with the frame it was chosen from, (7) send the move. Then every stored (frame, action) pair is exactly what the deployed policy will see, and no interpolation (UMI-style) is needed because nothing moves between capture and decision.
- (Inference) Per-step record to store: `episode_id`, `step_index`, `t_frame_captured`, `t_cmd_sent`, `t_cmd_returned`, joint angles and TCP pose before and after the move, the action (dx, dy in the sheet frame or a discrete class), who chose it (human, policy, perturbation), and a `done` flag; per-episode: sheet id, start index along the line, initial lateral offset and rotation, operator id, date, lighting note, success flag, reason for failure, and the collection round (demo, DART-perturbed, DAgger round k). Write the same data into a LeRobot v3 dataset (`observation.images.wrist`, `observation.state`, `action`, `timestamp`, `task`) so `lerobot-train` and the dataset visualiser can be used without conversion.
- (Inference) Label the end explicitly: the last step of every successful episode is a "stop" action taken from the frame that shows the puck at the end marker, and one or two extra frames after it (Isaac Lab buffer advice); a policy that must also decide when to stop needs positive examples of stopping, and a fixed step budget (for example 1.5x the demonstrated length) should be the safety cut-off at test time.
- (Inference) Episode boundaries: never append frames across a reset; a reset phase (moving the puck back, repositioning the sheet) must not be recorded as part of the episode.

### Gaps
- No source found that addresses stale-frame handling for a buffered JPEG stream specifically; the drain-and-timestamp rule above is engineering inference from the UMI latency-alignment principle.
- No manipulation-IL source found on learned stop signals; the "Done action" precedent is from adversarial IL in simulation.

## Key question 7: Augmentation and relabelling tricks that reduce the number of real demos

### Takeaway
Random crop (pixel shift) is the one augmentation with hard numbers in manipulation IL (removing it dropped robomimic's real Can success from 73.3 to 26.7 percent), colour jitter is standard but should stay mild, and the DAVE-2 shifted-viewpoint relabelling (older, 2016) is the direct precedent for synthesising "puck off the line, steer back" examples from a forward-looking camera; mirror flipping with swapped left/right actions is a 2026-supported way to double turn data if the camera and pusher are centred.

### Cited Findings
- robomimic (2021, older): removing pixel-shift (random crop) augmentation caused 47 percent (Square) and 35 percent (Transport) relative drops in simulation and dropped the real Can task from 73.3 percent to 26.7 percent; removing the wrist camera dropped real Can from 73.3 to 43.3 percent. Crop sizes: 76x76 from 84x84, 108x108 from 120x120, 216x216 from 240x240 (about 90 percent of the side). — [arXiv 2108.03298](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.03298v2)
- Diffusion Policy (2023): random crop 84x84 from 96x96 in simulation and 288x216 from 320x240 on the real robot. — [arXiv 2303.04137](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.04137v5)
- LeRobot image transforms (2026): ColorJitter (brightness, contrast, saturation, hue), SharpnessJitter, any torchvision v2 transform; disabled by default; "Start conservative: Begin with small ranges (e.g., brightness 0.9-1.1)"; "Strong augmentations can hurt performance if too aggressive"; up to 3 transforms per frame; applied only at training time. — [LeRobot dataset v3 docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/lerobot-dataset-v3)
- DAVE-2 / PilotNet (NVIDIA 2016, older): three cameras behind the windshield; off-centre views come from the left and right cameras, other shifts and rotations are simulated by viewpoint transformation of the nearest camera's image (assuming points below the horizon lie on flat ground); perturbations drawn from a zero-mean normal with standard deviation "twice the value measured from human drivers"; the label for each transformed image is adjusted "to one that correctly steers the vehicle back to the desired location and orientation in two seconds"; "Training with data from only the human driver is not sufficient" because the network must learn to recover from mistakes; about 72 hours of driving, sampled at 10 fps, with "a higher proportion of frames that represent road curves". — [NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/deep-learning-self-driving-cars/)
- The idea dates to ALVINN (Pomerleau, 1989): the training set was augmented with transformed images showing what the camera would see after drifting from the road centre, "coupled with a steering angle that would direct the vehicle back". — [NVIDIA PilotNet experiments](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.08776)
- Robust behavioural cloning for vehicles (2020, older): perspective-shift augmentation applied with probability 0.5 (left 0.25, right 0.25), feeding side-camera frames as centre frames with corrected steering labels, "to simulate high cross-track error without collecting data through unsafe manual driving". — [arXiv 2010.04767](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.04767)
- CCIL (Ke et al., ICLR 2024): fits a local dynamics model with Lipschitz continuity to the demos and generates corrective labels for states near but not in the data; reported to improve recovery from perturbations and compounding error in simulation including tabletop manipulation; needs only expert data, no interactive expert. — [arXiv 2310.12972](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.12972v2)
- MirrorDuo (2026): mirrored demonstration pairs as a data augmentation for BC or diffusion policy; "significantly improved performance under the same data budget when demonstrations are evenly distributed across both sides of the workspace". — [arXiv 2606.20048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.20048)
- RoCoDA (2024): SE(3) transforms of object poses with matching action adjustments; reports better sample efficiency than prior augmentation on five manipulation tasks. — [arXiv 2411.16959](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16959v2)
- SEIL: "image-level data augmentation is insufficient for an imitation learning agent to learn good manipulation policies in a reasonable amount of demonstrations"; equivariant model plus simulated transitions learned tasks within ten demos (simulation). — [SEIL via NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10487652)
- Collection-side augmentation: Lin et al. randomise gripper initial height and orientation; DART injects action noise; Isaac Lab "Action noise makes policies more robust"; MimicGen turns about 10 human demos into 1,000 generated ones with comparable policy performance. — [arXiv 2410.18647](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18647v3); [BAIR DART](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2017/10/26/dart/); [Isaac Lab docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/v2.0.2/source/overview/teleop_imitation.html)

### Inferences
- (Inference) The DAVE-2 trick maps almost one-to-one onto this camera: the image shows the line ahead with the puck at the bottom; shifting the image horizontally by k pixels (and optionally rotating it a few degrees) is what the camera would see if the puck were k pixels (a known number of millimetres, from a one-off ruler calibration) off the line; the relabelled action is "one step toward the line" (or the demonstrated action plus a lateral correction). This is a relabelling, so it must respect the same stepping rule used by the operators, and shifts should stay within the width the camera window allows (about plus or minus 2 cm of the 9 cm width).
- (Inference) Mirror flip: flipping the image left-right and swapping left and right actions is valid only if the pusher is centred under the camera and the camera is centred on the wrist; if the Niryo Vision Set camera is offset from the pusher axis, the mirrored image would not match a real mirrored state. Check on the robot before using it; if valid it doubles the data and balances left and right turns, which matters because the six sheets may be turn-biased.
- (Inference) Colour jitter must stay mild: the policy's cues are a red puck and a black line on white paper, so strong hue or saturation jitter could remove the very signal it needs; brightness and contrast within about 0.8 to 1.2 (LeRobot's conservative range) and a 90 percent random crop are the safe defaults.
- (Inference) Goal relabelling (hindsight) is not applicable: there is one goal (the end of the line) and no goal input to the policy. CCIL-style model-based corrective labels are more than a course project needs; the DAVE-2 shift relabelling and DART-style perturbation during collection cover the same purpose with far less engineering.

### Gaps
- No ablation found that isolates the shifted-viewpoint augmentation's contribution in DAVE-2 or PilotNet; the NVIDIA sources describe it and state its purpose, not its effect size.
- No real-robot manipulation study found that quantifies colour-jitter gains for a wrist camera; robomimic and Diffusion Policy report random crop only.
- CCIL's quantitative recovery results were not retrieved (only the method and claims).

## Cross-cutting decision summary (inference, built from the findings above)

- Primary collection: method (a) keyboard stepping, run through the same step-capture loop that the deployed policy will use; this gives the best observation match, a fixed action grid, and literal compliance with the professor's "we push the puck ourselves using the robot".
- Method (b) hand-guiding: use at most as a quick way to sketch a path, then replay it as 1 cm steps with the motors on to re-capture clean images (Li et al.); on its own it puts the operator's hand in the wrist camera's forward view and produces motion-blurred frames unlike test time.
- Method (c) scripted teacher: do not use as the demonstration source without the professor's explicit approval; if approved as an auxiliary, use it only to label recovery states near the line (Learning by Cheating, with the 2025 "realisability" caveat).
- Method (d) corrections: plan two to three HG-DAgger rounds ("policy proposes, human accepts or overrides"; label only human steps) after the first 100 to 150 episodes; plus DART-style random 1-step perturbations with human correction during ordinary collection.
- Budget: aim for 200 to 300 episodes spread over 20 to 30 printed paths with varied start points and lateral offsets, rather than 50 repeats on each of the six sheets (Lin et al. diversity result, LeRobot "10 per location").
- Quality: one written stepping rule for both students; discard failures and reversals; keep imperfect successes (robomimic BC-RNN benefited from weaker demos).
- Logging: capture-after-move with buffer flush, explicit stop step, LeRobot v3 format, the metadata list in Q6.
- Augmentation: 90 percent random crop and mild brightness/contrast jitter by default; DAVE-2-style shift-and-relabel and (if geometry allows) mirror flip as the two tricks most likely to cut the real-demo count.
