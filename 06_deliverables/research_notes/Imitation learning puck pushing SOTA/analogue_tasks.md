# Analogue tasks: visual line/lane following by imitation, and learned planar pushing (as of 2026)

Scope note. Our task: a Niryo Ned2 pushes a 2.4 cm red wooden puck along a 1 cm black line on A4 to the line's end, using only a wrist camera (640x480, about 9 fps) looking straight ahead, seeing a window about 9 cm wide and 7 cm ahead of the puck, puck at the bottom of every frame; one 1 cm blocking tool step per second; imitation learning only; 100 to 300 demonstration episodes; final test on an unseen path (straight line to arc, S-curve with hairpin, meander, self-crossing loops). Every inference below is written against that use case. Years are given per result. "Search snippet" means the page itself could not be fetched (HTTP 403) and only the search engine's extract was available; treat those as lower confidence.

## Key question 1: Visual lane/line following learned by imitation (PilotNet/DAVE-2, DAgger, Duckietown/AI-DO, DonkeyCar/JetBot/line followers)

### Takeaway
Behaviour cloning of a small CNN from a single forward camera reliably follows a track when (a) the training set is a few thousand to ~20 k frames that cover the test-time curvature and lighting, (b) recovery data is present (shifted-camera synthesis, deliberate off-centre starts, kept imperfect trajectories, or DAgger), and (c) evaluation is closed-loop. Plain BC without recovery data fails at rarely visited states (sharp corners, track edges); online/DAgger-style corrections fixed this in every real-robot study found (AutoRally 2018, HG-DAgger 2019, Duckietown 2022). Networks of 250 k parameters on 60x80 to 200x66 inputs are typical, so a laptop CPU is sufficient for our 1 step/s loop.

### Cited Findings

PilotNet / DAVE-2 (NVIDIA, 2016)
- About 72 hours of human driving were used for training; three windshield cameras were used for collection, one centre camera for deployment; training frames were sampled at 10 fps — [Bojarski et al. 2016, arXiv 1604.07316](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1604.07316)
- Network: 9 layers (1 fixed normalisation, 5 convolutional, 3 fully connected); first three convs 5x5 stride 2, last two 3x3; about 250 thousand parameters and 27 million connections; output is inverse turning radius 1/r (continuous regression) — [Bojarski et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1604.07316)
- Shifted-camera augmentation: off-centre views come from the left/right cameras; further shifts and rotations are synthesised by viewpoint transformation assuming flat ground; perturbation magnitude is normal with zero mean and a standard deviation twice that measured from human drivers; "the steering label for transformed images is adjusted to one that would steer the vehicle back to the desired location and orientation in two seconds" — [Bojarski et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1604.07316)
- Data selection: only lane-keeping frames are used, and frames on curves are oversampled to counter straight-road bias — [Bojarski et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1604.07316)
- Autonomy metric: autonomy = (1 − interventions × 6 s / elapsed time) × 100; in simulation an intervention is triggered when the vehicle departs the centre line by more than 1 m; reported about 98 % autonomy on a typical drive, 10 miles with zero interventions on the Garden State Parkway; turns between roads and lane changes were excluded from the metric — [Bojarski et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1604.07316)
- Stated limitations: viewpoint-transform artefacts grow with perturbation magnitude; the predecessor DAVE averaged about 20 m between crashes in complex environments — [Bojarski et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1604.07316)
- NVIDIA's 2020 retrospective restates that the left and right cameras "recorded a view equivalent to that of the centre camera as if the vehicle had been shifted left or right in the driving lane" and that all images were viewpoint-transformed over a range — [NVIDIA PilotNet Experiments 2020, arXiv 2010.08776](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.08776) (search snippet)

DAgger and online corrections in driving
- AutoRally (2018): 1/5-scale car on a ~30 m x 3 m elliptical dirt track, monocular camera rescaled to 160x80 RGB, ~10 M-parameter CNN, continuous steering and throttle, 50 Hz sampling, MPC expert. Batch IL with 3 000 / 6 000 / 9 000 / 12 000 samples completed 100 % / 51 % / 53 % / 69 % of 60 s runs; online IL (DAgger, 1–3 iterations totalling 6 000–12 000 samples) completed 100 % in every case at expert speed (~6 m/s). The batch policy "crashed into the lower-left boundary", an area "rarely explored in the expert's demonstrations" — [Pan et al. RSS 2018, arXiv 1709.07174](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.07174)
- HG-DAgger (2019): real test vehicle weaving between stationary cars; each method started from BC on 10 000 expert labels, then five epochs of 2 000 labels each. Over the first 5 000 evaluation samples on the real car: BC 11 collisions / 6 road departures, DAgger 11 / 1, HG-DAgger 0 / 0. Plain DAgger's instability is attributed to β decay and "perceived actuator lag" degrading the human's labels when control is shared — [Kelly et al. ICRA 2019, arXiv 1810.02890](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.02890)
- Monocular reactive UAV (2013): DAgger with 3 iterations per environment; the pilot labelled offline while the video was "replayed at slower speed (3 times slower than real-time)"; a linear ridge-regression controller on hand-crafted features (no deep net) flew 100 m without crashing in a park and avoided 62 % of trees in a dense forest at 1.5 m/s (pilot: 66 %) — [Ross et al. ICRA 2013, arXiv 1211.1690](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1211.1690)
- Student abstract (2021): on a simple road task "treating steering as classification gave much better results than regression", and DAgger "outperforms behaviour cloning given smaller data" — [Berntsson 2021](https://digitala-utstallningen.ungaforskare.se/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/GA_Abstract-alexander-berntsson.pdf) (search snippet; student work, small study)

Duckietown and the AI Driving Olympics
- AI-DO 1 (NeurIPS 2018): the imitation-learning lane-following baseline was a TensorFlow behaviour-cloning model trained on about 17 hours of logged human driving (Section 5 says ~16 h); its own scores are not reported; the organisers write that IL policies "are frequently very skilled at driving" (anecdotal) and that they were "unaware of any competitor that made use of the" logs — [Paull et al. 2019, arXiv 1903.02503](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.02503)
- AI-DO 1 finals on real Duckiebots: "Most runs however did not travel more than three tiles"; the winner's cumulative score was 37 s / 18 tiles; failures were attributed to sim-to-real differences (lighting, crowd noise), overfitting to the simulator, and parameters that were sensitive to the specific robot — [Paull et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.02503)
- AI-DO 2019 lane-following winner (2020): 20 294 training images (real logs + own real recordings + two simulated maps), 8 698 validation images; 640x480 input, top third removed, resized to 64x32 YUV; 5 conv + 2 FC CNN "based on Bojarski et al. (2016)" that runs on one CPU; outputs two continuous wheel voltages in [−1, 1]; trained with MSE; "imperfect trajectories (e.g. slightly leaving the lane) were kept, to teach recovery"; sequential multi-frame input "has not improved the performance" — [Sazanovich et al. 2020, arXiv 2007.03514](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.03514)
- Same paper, closed-loop results: on a simulated map not used in training (15 s limit) HYBRID 9.71 tiles, REAL-only 8.84, SIM-only 11.51, classic control 6.83; on a real robotarium (60 s) HYBRID 19.28 tiles, REAL 15.75, classic 11.36, SIM 2.48; in an unseen real environment (two scenarios, 30 s) HYBRID 30 tiles with no infractions, REAL-only 9 tiles (failed scenario 2), classic 10 tiles (failed scenario 2) — [Sazanovich et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.03514)
- Duckietown BC vs DAgger vs GAIL (2022): BC trained on 128 episodes x 768 steps = 98 304 samples; DAgger added 128 x 512 = 65 536 expert-labelled samples (163 840 total); images downscaled from 480x640 to 60x80; continuous throttle [0, 1] and steering [−1, 1]; Adam 1e-4, batch 32. Simulation (survival / distance / lateral deviation / infractions): BC w/o DR 15 / 5.44 / 0.75 / 0.63; DAgger w/o DR 15 / 5.67 / 0.63 / 0; GAIL 13.55 / 4.78 / 0.71 / 1.27. On the real Duckiebot the DAgger model without any sim-to-real transfer survived 1 s and 4.5 tiles ("completely failed"), while the domain-randomised model survived the full 60 s and 20 tiles — [Lőrincz et al. ICLR-W 2022, arXiv 2206.10797](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.10797)
- Same paper: training used multiple maps with roadside objects to give "robustness on unseen track layouts", but no held-out-map evaluation is reported — [Lőrincz et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.10797)
- SmartLab (BME) write-up on AI-DO 3: "Behavioral Cloning failed to solve the Duckietown lane following challenge, I could achieve an excellent result with the DAgger algorithm", placing first in the simulator category — [SmartLab AI, Medium](https://smartlabai.medium.com/imitation-learning-in-the-duckietown-environment-dddd2355783d) (search snippet; page returned 403 twice)

DonkeyCar
- Official guide: data is recorded 20 times per second; "We want about 10,000 of these" records; remove driving mistakes (off-track, crashes) from the data; indoor tracks with controlled lighting are better suited to the deep-learning autopilot than variable outdoor lighting — [DonkeyCar docs, Create an Autopilot](https://docs.donkeycar.com/guide/train_autopilot/)
- Vendor/community guide: practise until you can drive 10 laps cleanly, then record 10–20 laps (5–20 k images) — [Waveshare DonkeyCar wiki](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/DonkeyCar_for_Jetson_Nano-Collect_Data)
- Model choices: the "categorical" model bins steering and throttle and trains with cross-entropy, is described as "very robust" and good on limited compute, but "suffers from some arbitrary limitations of the chosen limits for number of categories"; the "linear" model gives continuous outputs — [DonkeyCar Keras parts docs](https://docs.donkeycar.com/parts/keras/)
- Real DonkeyCar speed/delay study (2023/24): Donkey Car S1 with a 160° wide-angle camera on a 17 m lap; ~19–20 k frames per speed condition at 20 Hz; default 5 conv + 3 dense CNN; on-policy metric = wall collisions per 10 laps. A model trained at slow speed had 0 infractions per 10 laps at slow speed and 10 at fast speed; the fast-trained model had 2 at fast and 16 at slow. Failure modes: fast-trained models driven slowly "cut into the inside of turns", slow-trained models driven fast "turned too late and hit the outside wall". Predicting the label ~50 ms into the future gave the fastest safe lap. No unseen-track evaluation — [Combating the effects of speed and delays, arXiv 2312.06670](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06670)
- Learning-to-Drive benchmark (2020): Donkey car, raw 120x160 images converted to grayscale, top 40 rows cropped, resized to 40x40; 20 Hz control. IL baseline: 10 K steps of human driving with a gamepad, categorical model with five conv + two FC layers; lap time 6.62 s versus human 5.82 s. Three tracks; Track 3 "only consists of sharp right-angle bends". Cross-track transfer (RL agents, % of max return): Track-1-trained agents scored 100 % on Tracks 2 and 3; Track-3-trained agents scored 21.7 % / 22.3 % (SAC) and 24.98 % (Dreamer on Track 2) on other tracks; lighting differences between training and test "increased variance" — [Viitala et al. 2020, arXiv 2008.00715](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.00715)

JetBot / small line followers
- JetBot road following is a regression: a target point (x, y) is placed on each live frame with a gamepad and a ResNet-18 predicts the steering coordinates; the notebook sets no required image count — [UAEU ELEC 395 week 12 (NVIDIA JetBot notebook mirror)](https://faculty.uaeu.ac.ae/mffbataineh/week12.html)
- A verified JetBot regression dataset consisted of 385 images at 224x224 — [Flinkow et al. 2024, arXiv 2411.14163](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14163); a forum user "retrained with 500 photos and still doesn't work" — [NVIDIA forums](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/road-folowing-with-the-jetracer/110981)
- A three-class (straight/left/right) CNN path follower reached 82 % classification accuracy and navigated the course in all 40 trials; the authors noted "numerical accuracy of the network was not the best indicator of how well it would imitate human operation" — [West Point, Application of CNN Image Classification for a Path-Following Robot](https://athena.westpoint.edu/items/bc2ccc8c-83ab-4155-a2f7-8c028b9e92b8) (search snippet; page returned 403)

Offline vs online evaluation
- Correlation between offline steering MSE and online success rate was |r| = 0.39 with a centre camera and no action noise, 0.54 with action noise, and 0.77 when validating on three-camera data; success rate and average completion correlated 0.8, but km-between-infractions correlated only 0.44 with completion — [Codevilla et al. ECCV 2018, arXiv 1809.04843](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04843)

### Inferences
- Data budget. At one frame per 1 cm step and 20–60 steps per path, 100–300 episodes yields roughly 3 000–15 000 labelled frames. That is the same order as the DonkeyCar 10 k-record target, the L2D 10 K-step IL baseline and the AI-DO winner's 20 k images, all of which produced working closed-loop followers on a fixed track. So the budget is adequate for a small CNN, provided the frames cover the curvatures and lighting of the test sheet (inference from the DonkeyCar, L2D and Sazanovich numbers).
- Recovery data is the single most consistent "what worked". Every real-robot study that compared plain BC with recovery/online data found plain BC failing at under-visited states (AutoRally corner, HG-DAgger departures, Duckietown DAgger vs BC). For us the cheapest equivalent of PilotNet's shifted camera is physical: start a fraction of episodes with the tool deliberately offset 1–2 cm sideways or 15–30° off the line's tangent and let the demonstrator correct; keep those frames with corrective labels. Because the camera is rigid to the tool and the puck is always at the bottom, a pure image shift/rotation of the frame with a "steer back" label (PilotNet's two-second rule) is also feasible, but the synthetic frames will not show the puck's true contact geometry, so physical offsets are safer.
- Discrete classes fit our action space. Our control is one 1 cm step; the natural output is a small set of step directions (e.g., 3, 5 or 8 headings), which is exactly the DonkeyCar "categorical" model and the L2D IL baseline, both reported as robust. Classification also allows class-balancing (PilotNet oversampled curves) and a confidence readout. Regression of a heading angle is equally supported (PilotNet, AI-DO winner) but gains little when the executed action is quantised to 1 cm anyway.
- Keep the network small and the image small. Working followers used 64x32 (AI-DO winner), 40x40 grayscale (L2D), 60x80 (Duckietown 2022) and 160x80 (AutoRally) inputs with 5-conv CNNs. Downscaling our 640x480 frame to about 64x48–160x120 and a 5-conv network is in line with practice and trains on a laptop CPU.
- Offline accuracy will mislead. The 0.39 correlation (Codevilla) and the West Point remark mean we must grade models by closed-loop runs on the robot, not by validation accuracy.
- Lighting: fix the lab lighting for both demonstration and test sessions (DonkeyCar docs; AI-DO finals; L2D variance), or deliberately vary it during demonstrations.

### Gaps
- No official numeric scores for the Duckietown IL baseline (the AI-DO paper does not report them).
- The SmartLab/BME DAgger write-up and the West Point paper could not be fetched (403); numbers come from search snippets only.
- A search result mentioned a 2026 review in which raising a miniature-car training set from ~1 346 to 5 351 images cut the error rate from 36 % to 3 %, but the search engine did not attach a URL I could verify, so it is not listed as a finding.
- No study was found that trained a line follower by BC and then tested on an unseen line layout with a lateral-deviation metric; the closest is the L2D cross-track table (RL agents, not IL) and the AI-DO winner's unseen real environment (tiles, not deviation).

## Key question 2: Learned pushing of an object with a point/rod pusher

### Takeaway
Pushing with a rod is intrinsically stochastic (5–12 % translational and 1–4° rotational scatter on identical 15 cm pushes), so successful learned pushers are closed-loop and visual. Imitation-learning pushers that work on real hardware (Push-T 2023, eye-in-hand DMD 2024, sim-and-real cotraining 2025) use end-effector position targets as actions, images from a wrist and/or overhead camera, 24–150 real demonstrations, and judge success by a final-pose tolerance (1–1.5 cm, 3.5°) or IoU with the goal. Changing the object's direction requires repositioning the contact point, which the controllers handle either through contact-mode switching (sticking/sliding) or because the demonstrator did it.

### Cited Findings

Physics and variability of rod pushing
- MIT push dataset (2016): stainless cylindrical rod pusher 9.5 mm diameter; 11 objects, 4 surfaces, 10 speeds, 9 push directions; ~6 000 pushes per object–surface pair, over one million pusher/object/force triples. Over 2 000 repeated identical straight pushes of a rectangle (15 cm at 20 mm/s), the final-pose standard deviation was 3.4–11.7 mm translational (5.2–12.5 %) and 1.3–4.5° rotational depending on surface, and the distribution was multimodal and "clearly not Gaussian" — [Yu, Bauza, Fazeli, Rodriguez IROS 2016, arXiv 1604.04038](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1604.04038)
- Same dataset: friction varied across the surface (std 0.016–0.064) and fell 11–22 % during a 100-scan break-in on three of four materials; the ellipsoidal limit-surface assumption is "only a rough approximation" — [Yu et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1604.04038)
- Omnipush (2019): 250 objects x 250 pushes with RGB-D video, intended for studying generalisation of learned pushing dynamics to unseen objects — [Bauza et al. IROS 2019, arXiv 1910.00618](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00618)
- Hybrid-MPC pusher-slider (2017/2020): contact modes are sticking, sliding left and sliding right; a point pusher tracked a figure-8 of two 0.15 m-radius circles at 0.05 m/s with "a small steady-state error"; after a hand perturbation "the pusher reacts to reduce the error by following a fast sliding motion to stabilize the object and then push it back towards the desired trajectory using a sticking phase"; the convex MPC ran at 250 Hz — [Hogan, Grau, Rodriguez 2017, arXiv 1710.05724](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1710.05724)

Learned pushing: action representation and results
- Learning to Poke by Poking (2016): Baxter, 400+ hours, >100 K pokes on 16 objects; action = start point, angle, length, discretised to a 20x20 grid, 36 angle bins and 11 length bins (11th = "no poke"); inverse model from current + goal image; greedy replanning up to 10 pokes. Failure modes: objects "flip mid-poke and move the wrong way", greedy planning "produced zig-zag object paths rather than straight ones", and pose control "is not satisfactory", attributed partly to "large, discrete pokes rather than smaller, continuous ones" — [Agrawal et al. NeurIPS 2016, arXiv 1606.07419](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.07419)
- Push-T / Diffusion Policy (2023): circular pusher, T-shaped block; action = end-effector position (position control "suits it better than velocity control"); commands at 10 Hz interpolated to 125 Hz; 200 sim demos and 136 real demos; real success = final IoU above the minimum IoU in the human demos. Real results: Diffusion Policy end-to-end CNN 95 % success (IoU 0.80 vs human 0.84), LSTM-GMM 10–20 %, IBC 0 %. Failure modes: LSTM-GMM "got stuck near the T block in 8 of 20", IBC "left the T block prematurely in 6 of 20"; idle actions left in training data made baselines overfit small actions — [Chi et al. RSS 2023, arXiv 2303.04137](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.04137)
- Sim-and-real cotraining for planar pushing from pixels (2025): KUKA iiwa, T slider, cylindrical pusher; action = pusher x-y target (z and orientation fixed); observations = pusher pose + overhead RGB + wrist RGB; sim success = slider within 1.5 cm and 3.5° of goal within 75 s; real success = "a human teleoperator would not readjust the final slider pose", 20 initial conditions, 90 s limit. Real-only policies: 10 demos 2/20, 50 demos 10/20, 150 demos 20/20; cotrained with 500–2 000 sim demos: 14/20 and 19/20 at 10 and 50 real demos — [Wei et al. 2025, arXiv 2503.22634](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.22634)
- Diffusion Meets DAgger, eye-in-hand (2024): wrist-mounted GoPro is the only observation; policy regresses relative end-effector motion; pushing task = reach a target object and push it "to the target location marked by a red circle"; recovery frames are synthesised as novel wrist views perturbed 2–4 cm in a random direction, labelled with the pose at frame t+3 because "labeling with t+1 causes overshooting". Pushing success: BC 30 % vs DMD 100 % with 24 demos; 20 % vs 80 % with 8 demos; an unseen cup 80 % vs 90 %; a NeRF-based alternative "pushed the apple off the table" — [Zhang et al. 2024, arXiv 2402.17768](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17768)
- Graph-structured visual imitation (2019): Baxter pushes an octagon along a demonstrated path; 4-D action (xyz + yaw), 0.4 s steps, 30-step episodes; success = object "within 1 cm of the desired target position" from starts perturbed within a 6 cm ball; straight-line pushing 5/5 (octagon) and 4/5 (square); a 90° direction-change path "requires the robot to change the point of contact with the object during pushing" — [Sieb et al. CoRL 2019, arXiv 1907.05518](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.05518)
- Student/simulated analogue: an ACT policy trained from webcam hand-teleoperation pushes a puck to a green ring, judged within 5 cm, in MuJoCo — [handrobot GitHub](https://github.com/guptabhishekumar/handrobot)
- Ark framework (2025) demonstrates a diffusion-policy pushing task on a ViperX300s with an RGB camera and joint-position actions — [Ark, arXiv 2506.21628](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.21628) (search snippet)

### Inferences
- Expect scatter of a few millimetres per 1 cm step. Scaling the MIT numbers (5–12 % of push length) to our 1 cm steps gives sub-millimetre scatter per step but it compounds over a 20–40 cm path; the multimodality (puck sliding off one side of the rod) is the real risk. Closed-loop visual correction every step, which our 9 fps camera and 1 step/s loop already provide, is how all the cited learned pushers cope.
- A round puck removes the orientation problem (no yaw to control, unlike the T block), but a bare rod contacting a disc off-centre makes the puck slide along the line from contact point through its centre, so lateral error of the tool turns into lateral drift of the puck. The demonstrations must therefore show the demonstrator re-centring the rod behind the puck (a sideways or backward step) rather than only stepping forward.
- Direction changes need contact repositioning. Both the model-based (sliding mode, Hogan) and the imitation (change contact point, Sieb) results say a hairpin cannot be followed by forward steps alone. Our action set should include sideways and backward 1 cm steps, and the expert must actually use them on hairpins so they appear in the data.
- Action representation: every real-hardware IL pusher used an end-effector position target (absolute or relative), not joint targets or velocities. Our "1 cm tool step in direction d" is a relative position target and matches that practice.
- Demonstration count: with a plain policy, 50 real demos gave 10/20 and 150 gave 20/20 on Push-T-style pushing (2025), and 24 demos gave 30 % with BC but 100 % with synthesised recovery views (2024). Our 100–300 episodes sits in the range where real-only training reached high success, provided recovery states are covered.
- Remove idle frames. Push-T baselines overfit to idle actions left in the data; with blocking 1 cm steps we should log exactly one frame per step and avoid "no-move" labels.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that pushes a disc with a bare rod using only a wrist camera and imitation learning; the closest combine a wrist camera with an overhead camera (2025) or use a GoPro wrist camera with a short reach-and-push task (2024).
- Omnipush's generalisation-to-unseen-object numbers were not on the abstract page and were not fetched.
- The Hogan MPC paper gives no millimetre tracking error.

## Key question 3: Tasks combining both: pushing an object along a drawn path or trajectory

### Takeaway
Pushing along a demonstrated or drawn path exists in a handful of research systems (single-video imitation on Baxter 2019, drawing-based imitation L2D2 2025, waypoint extraction VIEW 2024) and as model-based figure-8 tracking (2017/2020), but no study or student project was found that learns, end to end from a wrist camera, to push an object along a line printed on the surface. Our task therefore has to borrow its data and evaluation recipe from lane following and its contact handling from pushing.

### Cited Findings
- Baxter pushed an octagon along a human-demonstrated path including a straight line and a 90° turn, the latter requiring a change of contact point; success within 1 cm; 5/5 and 4/5 on straight pushes — [Sieb et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.05518)
- L2D2 (2025): users draw trajectories on a tablet over an image of the workspace; a push task had the user "draw a path to move a bowl to a target region" — [Mehta et al. 2025, arXiv 2505.12072](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.12072) (search snippet)
- VIEW (2024): learns pushing and pick-and-place from a single video by extracting waypoints, reportedly with fewer than 20 real-world trials — [VIEW, Autonomous Robots 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10514-024-10188-y) (search snippet)
- Model-based figure-8 tracking with a point pusher at 0.05 m/s, including recovery from hand perturbations — [Hogan et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1710.05724)
- Weld-seam tracking is the industrial version of "camera ahead of the tool follows a line": a passive-vision segmentation tracker (BiSeNetV2) computed the seam-to-torch offset with an average error within 0.07 mm at 57 fps — [Metals 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/12/8/1365) (search snippet); a Mask R-CNN seam extractor on an inspection robot reported a maximum drift of 3° and 10 mm — [Drones 2022](https://doaj.org/article/aff6105b875e483680c8f256ef9fa35b) (search snippet). These are classical perception-plus-controller pipelines, not imitation learning.
- GitHub/LeRobot community: the closest repos are pick-and-place with ACT or diffusion on SO-ARM arms with a wrist camera (e.g. a wrist-only ACT policy with 6-D actions — [rsundaraws/act_arm2_wristonly](https://huggingface.co/rsundaraws/act_arm2_wristonly)) and a real-arm BC overfitting post-mortem ("kept grasping at a memorized position" — [lerobot-project](https://github.com/Telfair8632/lerobot-project)); no push-along-a-line project was found.

### Inferences
- The seam-tracking literature confirms that "line ahead of the tool, tool at the bottom of the frame" is a workable geometry for lateral correction, but it reaches sub-millimetre error only with explicit segmentation; an end-to-end BC model will be coarser, which is acceptable for a 1 cm line and a 2.4 cm puck.
- Because no end-to-end precedent exists, the safest design copies the two best-evidenced parts: a lane-following-style small CNN with recovery data and closed-loop metrics (Q1), and pushing-style relative position actions that include sideways/backward moves for contact repositioning (Q2).
- The LeRobot overfitting post-mortem is a warning for 100–300 episodes: low training loss did not mean a working policy; the model memorised positions. Evaluate on the robot early, on a held-out sheet.

### Gaps
- No student or course project with a robot arm pushing along a line was found on GitHub, YouTube or Hackster via the queries tried ("push object along path imitation learning", "line following manipulation", "robot arm push object along drawn line", "puck pushing behavior cloning"). This is a negative result, not proof of absence.
- The L2D2 and VIEW pages were not fetched; numbers come from search snippets.

## Key question 4: Metrics used in the analogues and how unseen tracks/paths were chosen

### Takeaway
Driving analogues grade closed-loop runs by distance/completion, survival time, lateral deviation and interventions or infractions (several at once, because they correlate only 0.44–0.8 with each other); pushing analogues grade by final-pose tolerance or IoU and binary success over 20 trials. Unseen tests were a held-out map or environment (AI-DO winner), a different-shaped track (L2D), or randomised start poses (Push-T, DMD); nobody held out a path by curvature class except L2D, whose all-sharp-bend track was the one that did not transfer.

### Cited Findings
- AI-DO lane following: traveled distance along the discretised lane (circling or wrong lane does not count), survival time (ends when the car leaves the road or hits an obstacle), median lateral deviation from the centre line, and major infractions (time outside drivable zones); median over five runs; ranking by distance, ties broken by survival, deviation, infractions; simulation evaluation used 20 episodes on only one map, which the authors call statistically insufficient — [Paull et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.02503)
- PilotNet autonomy = (1 − interventions x 6 s / elapsed) x 100, intervention when >1 m off centre in simulation; test set was ~3 hours / 100 miles of prerecorded driving on roads of several types — [Bojarski et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1604.07316)
- Offline-to-online: success rate and average completion correlate 0.8; km between infractions correlates 0.44 with completion; "advisable to measure several online metrics" — [Codevilla et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04843)
- AI-DO 2019 winner: closed-loop tiles in a fixed time (15 s sim, 60 s and 30 s real), with the simulated map "not used in training" and a final real test in an "unseen environment" with two scenarios — [Sazanovich et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.03514)
- DonkeyCar study: on-policy metric = wall collisions ("infractions") over 10 laps; off-policy = MAE — [arXiv 2312.06670](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06670)
- L2D: success as % of maximum return, mean ± std over five episodes, trained on one track and tested on the other two — [Viitala et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.00715)
- Push-T real: success = final IoU above the minimum IoU in the human demonstrations; 20 evaluations per method — [Chi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.04137)
- Cotraining pushing: sim success within 1.5 cm and 3.5° in 75 s; real success judged by a teleoperator over 20 initial conditions with a 90 s limit — [Wei et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.22634)
- Visual imitation pushing: success within 1 cm of target from starts perturbed in a 6 cm ball, 5 runs — [Sieb et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.05518)
- DMD pushing: binary success over randomised start locations, 8–24 demos — [Zhang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17768)

### Inferences
- A metric set for our task that mirrors AI-DO and Codevilla: (1) completion fraction = arc length of line covered before failure / total length; (2) survival = steps until the puck centre is more than a threshold (e.g., 1.5 cm, roughly half a puck) off the line; (3) median lateral deviation of the puck centre from the line, measured from an overhead photo or marks; (4) interventions or a binary success at the line's end within a stated tolerance (1–1.5 cm as in the pushing papers); (5) steps taken versus the demonstrator's steps. Report all five, with the median of at least 5 runs per sheet, since 20 runs per policy is the norm in pushing papers.
- Choosing the unseen path: following the L2D result, the training sheets must contain the tightest curvature that the test sheet contains; a test sheet that only has new sequencing of seen curvature classes (arc, S, hairpin, meander) is a fair generalisation test, whereas a test sheet with a tighter hairpin than any training sheet is likely to fail. Evaluate also with randomised puck start offsets, as the pushing papers do.

### Gaps
- No analogue reports lateral deviation for a pushed object along a path; the Hogan MPC paper reports only qualitative "small steady-state error".

## Key question 5: Narrow forward-looking cameras on sharp turns

### Takeaway
The one study that quantified it (reactive UAV, 2013) found the narrow field of view to be the largest single failure cause (29.3 % of failures) and offered no fix beyond proposing memory and a wider view; driving systems avoided the problem with wide-angle lenses (DonkeyCar 160°), oversampled curves (PilotNet), excluded turns from the metric (PilotNet), or lookahead labels (predict 50 ms ahead; label with pose at t+3). Multi-frame input helped turns in one simulation study but not in the AI-DO winner's real-world tests.

### Cited Findings
- "the narrow field-of-view was the largest contributor to failures of the reactive approach"; 29.3 % of failures were due to a too-narrow FOV; the typical failure is that after avoiding one tree, "as it turns a new tree comes into view" and the drone collides sideways; "This problem inevitably afflicts purely reactive controllers"; proposed remedies were memory of recent visual features or higher-level reasoning and a wider camera FOV; the controller already appended a low-pass command history and yaw deviation to the image features — [Ross et al. 2013](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1211.1690)
- PilotNet oversampled curve frames and excluded turns between roads from its autonomy figure — [Bojarski et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1604.07316)
- DonkeyCar study used a 160° wide-angle camera; turn failures were timing errors (turning too early/late for the speed), and predicting the label about 50 ms into the future gave the fastest safe laps — [arXiv 2312.06670](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06670)
- The AI-DO 2019 winner tried sequential multi-frame input and it "has not improved the performance" — [Sazanovich et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.03514)
- In a simulated urban study a CNN-LSTM outperformed the CNN in turns following high-speed stretches — [Autonomous Vehicle Control: End-to-end Learning in Simulated Urban Environments 2019, arXiv 1905.06712](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06712) (search snippet); a 2024 paper reports that adding visual memory and kinematic inputs "enhances the quality of the final control behavior for following the lane" — [Neurocomputing 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925231224009329) (search snippet)
- Eye-in-hand recovery labels: using the pose at t+1 as the label "causes overshooting, where the label points away from the goal", so labels were taken from t+3 — [Zhang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17768)
- L2D: the track made only of sharp right-angle bends was the one whose agents did not transfer (21–25 % on other tracks), while agents trained on the mixed track transferred at 100 % — [Viitala et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.00715)
- Contact repositioning on direction change is required for pushing a 90° turn — [Sieb et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.05518); the model-based controller handles it by a sliding-mode manoeuvre — [Hogan et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1710.05724)

### Inferences
- Geometry of our window. With 7 cm of line visible ahead and 1 cm steps, the policy sees at most about 7 steps of future line. A hairpin whose continuation lies outside a 9 cm-wide, 7 cm-deep window is the exact "new tree comes into view only after turning" situation Ross described; a purely reactive policy will turn late. This is the task's main predicted failure mode (inference from Ross 2013 and L2D).
- Mitigations with evidence behind them, in order of cost: (1) include many hairpin and tight-arc episodes in the demonstrations and oversample them in training (PilotNet); (2) label each frame with the demonstrator's move a few steps ahead or with the mean of the next k moves rather than the immediate move (DonkeyCar 50 ms, DMD t+3), which makes the model commit to a turn while the curve is still visible; (3) add the last one to three moves as extra input (Ross's command history), which is cheap and does not require a recurrent network; (4) frame stacking or an LSTM is plausible but the real-world evidence is mixed (helped in simulation, no gain in the AI-DO winner), so test it only if (1)–(3) fail.
- At a hairpin the puck itself is the problem, not only the view: the rod must step round the puck to push it back the other way, and during that manoeuvre the line may be partly hidden by the puck at the bottom of the frame. Demonstrations should show a consistent, repeatable repositioning move so the classifier sees one pattern, not many.
- Self-crossing loops: a crossing seen 7 cm ahead looks like two valid continuations; no analogue in the IL line-following literature handles this without an explicit command input (Duckietown intersections) or memory. Expect ambiguity; a heading-continuity prior (prefer the branch closest to straight ahead) is the obvious fix but is untested here.

### Gaps
- No lane-following study was found that measures failure rate as a function of camera field of view or curve radius; the FOV effect is quantified only for the UAV case.
- No evidence on how end-to-end line followers behave at self-crossings; the Duckietown intersection work uses explicit turn commands and was not covered here.
- The two memory-related papers (1905.06712, Neurocomputing 2024) were seen only as search snippets.
