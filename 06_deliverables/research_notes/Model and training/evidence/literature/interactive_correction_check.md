# Sceptical check: DAgger and human-gated corrections (key interactive_correction)

Checked on 9 November 2026. I opened every cited source myself. How I read each one is stated per claim.

How the reading was done:
- The web tool could not read the PDFs directly (compressed streams), so I extracted their text with a small script that uses only the Python standard library (`scratchpad/pdf_tools/extract_pdf_text.py`) and searched that text. Table digits below come from the PDF text wherever a PDF exists.
- HTML versions (arXiv HTML, ar5iv) were read through the web tool, which summarises with a small model; where a number mattered I asked for it again in a second, narrower request.
- Venue facts that I saw only in search-result listings (not on the publisher page) are marked "unchecked".
- No direct quotations are used in this note; everything is paraphrased.

Verdict words: supported (the source says this), corrected (the source says something different), not supported, could not open. "Checked" means I read it in the source.

## 2. Challenge to the conclusion

### 2.1 The evidence sentence

1. HG-DAgger against behaviour cloning on the car (0 against 6 road departures). The numbers are right and the comparison is matched on labels, which is good. But the test is five layouts and 5,000 samples, the authors themselves call it a heuristic, the obstacles were simulated, and the novice saw a hand-made low-dimensional state. It supports the mechanism, not vision-based control.
2. ThriftyDAgger 12/15 against behaviour cloning 0/15. Behaviour cloning was trained only on the 25 initial demonstrations (Appendix 7.2.2), so this mixes "more data" with "interactive data". In the same test the human-gated method got 10/15; 12 against 10 out of 15 is within chance.
3. At equal data, corrections beat extra demonstrations. The equal-data evidence is BC-Z (real robots, but 25,877 episodes and TPU training) and Mandlekar et al. (simulation, low-dimensional states, best-checkpoint scores). Plain HG-DAgger without balancing did not beat Full Demos on Threading (75.3 against 76.7). Further support the researcher did not cite: the HG-DAgger car test is matched on labels, and in ThriftyDAgger's simulation behaviour cloning got 50 percent more offline data and still scored 24/100 against 57/100 for HG-DAgger.
4. "Real-robot studies mostly used 1 to 3 rounds." Among the opened real-robot studies: 2 (Laskey), 3 (Sirius; drone, per setting), 1 to 3 batches (CR-DAgger), 5 (HG-DAgger), 16 (BC-Z), 24 short iterations (EIL car), an update after every episode (ThriftyDAgger). That is about half, not most. Few rounds is a sensible choice for a laptop CPU and a course, but no study shows it is best; the one direct test (Mandlekar et al., one operator, simulation) found iterating helped on the longer task.
5. "Steps just before a take-over are best excluded." Supported for weighted behaviour cloning (Sirius, simulation ablation; CR-DAgger sampling). EIL used them as negative examples, which needs a value-based learner. If the plan trains only on the human's recoveries (HG-DAgger style), those robot steps never enter the data, so the rule matters only if the model's own accepted steps are kept.
6. "Corrections need up-weighting or they are swamped." Supported in simulation (IWR against IWR without balancing; Sirius ablation peaking at one half) and by CR-DAgger Finding 4 on book flipping. But plain aggregation without up-weighting still helped in Kelly et al., BC-Z and ThriftyDAgger's HG-DAgger baseline. "Help" fits the evidence; "need" is too strong.
7. Labels typed afterwards were inconsistent. True for continuous mouse labels on half-speed video (Laskey et al.). Ross et al. used offline labelling successfully with one expert pilot and a linear policy (no head-to-head test). Discrete move names may be easier to label; untested.

### 2.2 The judgement, for this project

What holds up:
- Human-gated take-over at a pause suits the stop-and-go loop: the HG-DAgger worry about actuator lag and abrupt switching (and CR-DAgger's take-over speed spikes) largely disappears when the arm is stopped before the person acts.
- Hand-guided recoveries replayed with the existing tool carry the same move-name and exact-step labels as the demonstrations, which avoids the Laskey failure mode.
- Tagging, and retraining from scratch on all data (CR-DAgger Findings 4 and 5), suit a CPU better than per-episode updates.

Problems:
1. A round far smaller than in the evidence. The researcher's own estimate is about 750 correction pictures per round, about 4 percent of 18,500. The cited studies added 20 to 33 percent per round (HG-DAgger 2,000 of 10,000 labels; Sirius and Mandlekar one third; ThriftyDAgger calculated roughly 375 human actions against 1,381 initial pairs, about 27 percent, from counts over successful episodes). Calculated: 20 to 33 percent here would be 3,700 to 6,100 pictures. So per round, relative to the initial data, the plan is 5 to 8 times smaller than any cited study. In absolute terms (hundreds of human-labelled steps) it is similar to ThriftyDAgger and EIL, so it may still help, but the size of the gain is a guess.
2. Half of each batch is an extreme up-weight at 4 percent. Calculated: with 750 corrections and 18,500 demonstration pictures, a 50/50 batch draws each correction picture about 25 times as often as each demonstration picture (12.8 times relative to uniform sampling). In IWR and Sirius the corrections were a much larger share of the data (one third of the initial samples per round), so their per-picture up-weight was milder; the exact factor depends on robot-sample counts I did not find in the text. A 25-fold weight drifts towards fine-tuning on corrections, which CR-DAgger found unstable (minus 30 and minus 15.6 points). Suggestion: compare a correction share of about 10 to 25 percent with 50 percent and choose on held-out tracks.
3. The take-over rate is borrowed from successful episodes. The 1.4 to 1.6 interventions per episode in ThriftyDAgger were computed over successful episodes only (Section 5.1). A first model, especially on new practice tracks, will likely need more take-overs and some episodes will be abandoned.
4. The robot-time estimate is unmeasured and probably optimistic. The researcher's 2 minutes per correction must cover motors off, guiding, putting the puck back with the ghost overlay, returning the arm, motors on and the replay. My own guess (also a guess) is 3 to 5 minutes; with 75 corrections, 50 model runs (about 1.5 hours, calculated) and about 1 minute of reset per run (guess), a round would take about 6 to 8.5 hours. No source reports robot hours per round. A pilot of about 5 episodes would give the real numbers.
5. Unseen tracks. None of the opened studies tested whether correction data carry over to layouts outside the training distribution; their test sets were new starts from the same distribution (for example 30 held-out starts in Laskey et al., random obstacle layouts in Kelly et al.). Collecting on new practice tracks is sensible, but the benefit on the exam's unseen tracks is an extrapolation. Keep at least one practice track for evaluation only.
6. Sensing and compute differ. Of the positive real-robot results, HG-DAgger and EIL used hand-made low-dimensional states; Sirius two cameras plus proprioception with ResNet-18 encoders and an LSTM; CR-DAgger a wrist camera plus a force-torque sensor, trained on an RTX 4090; BC-Z one camera but 25,877 episodes on TPUs; ThriftyDAgger 64 x 64 workspace pictures. None trained on a laptop CPU. The closest analogue to this project is DMD's wrist-camera pushing with 1 cm steps, where off-track data raised success from 20 to 80 percent; that supports the need for recovery data, not human corrections in particular.
7. Finishing time. In EIL's real-car test, learning only from recoveries gave the jerkiest policy (Table II). Recovery-heavy data may push the model towards small or hesitant moves; measure steps per finished track as well as success.
8. Cheaper options not weighed. (a) The tool's planned "model plus key override" correction mode (project memory, project-recording-tool.md) gives immediate per-step corrections with move-name labels and no motors-off guiding, puck reset or replay; it fits the decision to train on move names first, though its exact-step labels would be the fixed key move. (b) Deliberate recovery demonstrations started off the line (DART-like; the 30.10.26 data-collection analysis in project memory recommended 30 percent offset starts; whether that was adopted is not recorded there) need no model in the loop; DART reports an average 62 percent gain (abstract only), while BC-Z dropped noise injection as too hard to demonstrate with. Check whether the 208 demonstrations already contain offset starts before planning a correction round.

Overall: the evidence supports "one or two human-gated correction rounds after a first working model usually help", and the proposed mechanism suits this robot. It does not support the specific numbers for this project (50 episodes being enough, a 50 percent batch share, 3 to 5 robot hours); these sit outside the tested regimes and should be measured in a small pilot first.

## 3. Evidence the researcher missed

1. ThriftyDAgger Appendix 7.2.2: the physical behaviour cloning baseline was trained only on the initial data (not matched); in simulation it got 50 percent more offline data (4,004 transitions) and still lost.
2. ThriftyDAgger Section 5.1: intervention and action counts are over successful episodes only, so they understate take-over rates for planning.
3. Kelly et al. Section III-A: the novice's input was a hand-made low-dimensional state, obstacles were simulated, and behaviour cloning got the same extra labels; footnote 1: HG-DAgger is unsuitable where the expert cannot quickly spot and react to danger (the stop-and-go loop satisfies this).
4. Mandlekar et al.: low-dimensional observations, LSTM policies, best-checkpoint scores over 3 seeds, large spreads; plain HG-DAgger no better than Full Demos on Threading.
5. Spencer et al. Table II and Observation 3: on the real car HG-DAgger reached zero collisions with 223 expert samples (behaviour cloning not after more than 1,680), but learning only from recoveries made the jerkiest policy.
6. Sirius: two cameras plus proprioception; real results from one seed with checkpoint screening on 5 trials.
7. CR-DAgger: RTX 4090, wrist camera plus force sensor, diffusion base policy; frequent small updates failed.
8. BC-Z: one head camera but 25,877 episodes on TPUs, 16 HG-DAgger iterations; DART-style noise tried and dropped.
9. DMD: the closest task analogue (wrist-camera pushing, 1 cm steps, 20 to 80 percent), but with extra play data for the diffusion model and no compute reported.
10. Ross et al. (drone): offline labelling on slowed video worked for one expert pilot, a counterpoint to Laskey et al.
11. No opened study measured robot or human hours per correction round, or transfer of correction data to unseen layouts.
12. Not opened (leads only): LazyDAgger and SafeDAgger (robot-gated baselines in ThriftyDAgger), Spencer et al. 2021 on regimes of covariate shift.
